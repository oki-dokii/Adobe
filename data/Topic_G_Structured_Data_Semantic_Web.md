## 0. Framing — what Topic G is actually for

Topic G is not "add JSON-LD" as trivia. It exists to answer one design question for the marketplace:

> **Beyond whether structured data is merely syntactically valid, does it accurately, completely, and currently represent what a page's visible content actually says — and is the specific failure mode of *technically-valid-but-content-mismatched* markup a distinct, higher-value, less-obvious signal than simple presence/absence or syntax checking, which most teams will default to?**

Every sub-topic below is evaluated against that question, not against "does this page have schema markup."

**Explicit boundary, established in Topic F and preserved here:** Topic F's `entity-identity-audit` owns the narrow slice of schema specifically bearing on entity identity — `Organization`/`Person`/`LocalBusiness` `sameAs`/`@id`/`alternateName`/`parentOrganization`. Topic G owns **general schema validity, correctness, completeness, and content-agreement** across all other schema.org types relevant to this hackathon's scope (`Product`, `Offer`, `Review`/`AggregateRating`, `Article`, `FAQPage`, `BreadcrumbList`, `Event`), independent of entity-identity concerns. This document does not re-litigate or duplicate Topic F's F13-F16/F32-F36 scaffolding sections; it assumes them as already-established shared context (Google's Organization documentation, the `sameAs`/`@id` Linked Data primitive, the Knowledge Graph API's schema.org-native design) and builds the general-schema investigation on top of that foundation.

The master research map lists G1–G33 under this topic. Following the same condense-and-focus discipline used across this document series, G1–G33 organizes into **two genuinely distinct investigation units**, because close reading shows the raw list is mostly enumerating (a) *which schema.org vocabulary* (G1–G20: Schema.org, JSON-LD, Microdata, RDFa, and the type/property list — Organization, WebSite, WebPage, BreadcrumbList, Article, Product, Offer, Review, FAQPage, Person, LocalBusiness, Event, sameAs, identifier, knowsAbout, about, mentions, author, publisher, datePublished, dateModified) exists as available machinery, versus (b) a small number of genuinely distinct *failure mechanisms* that machinery can exhibit (G26–G33: structured-data/page-content consistency, invalid/incomplete/contradictory/spammy structured data, JS-generated structured data, discoverability, validation):

1. **§1 — The technical/quality guideline split, and why content-mismatch is the load-bearing, underexploited mechanism.** Google's own primary documentation draws an explicit, formal line between *technical* guidelines (automatically testable) and *quality* guidelines (not automatically testable by any validator) — this split is the single most important, directly-sourced fact this document has, and it directly determines where this hackathon's actual detection opportunity lies.
2. **§2 — Completeness, specificity, and multi-item linking as machine-comprehension mechanisms** (the `@id`-linking pattern, required-vs-recommended property completeness, and the "must mark up ALL visible instances, not a subset" rule) — a distinct, less-obvious mechanism from simple presence/absence, directly useful for detection design.

**Constraint from the handout, re-read carefully:** the brief explicitly warns against assuming "missing structured data = failure." This document's central finding (§1) provides the concrete, primary-source-grounded reason *why* that assumption is wrong, and simultaneously identifies the more diagnostic, less-obvious check the brief's own research-priority list explicitly calls for ("passage/chunk quality," "false-positive reduction") that most teams checking for schema presence alone will miss entirely.

## 1. FACT vs OBSERVATION vs HYPOTHESIS vs INFERENCE vs SPECULATION — global legend

Same legend as Topics F, H, P, M, and T, used identically here:
- **FACT** — documented by the vendor/standards body, or established in peer-reviewed/archival literature.
- **OBSERVATION** — reported by independent researchers/practitioners running real, methodologically transparent trials (not vendor-confirmed).
- **HYPOTHESIS** — our proposed testable claim, not yet validated by us.
- **INFERENCE** — reasonable derivation from FACT/OBSERVATION, one logical step removed.
- **SPECULATION** — plausible but unconfirmed; flagged, not built on.

**A note on source quality control, continuing the pattern established across this document series — with an important, more positive twist this time.** As in every prior topic, this research pass surfaced a large cluster of SEO-content-marketing sites (Schema Engine AI, Penaltyhammer, Over The Top SEO, Cognitive SEO, Hillweb Creations, Digital Applied) discussing "structured data penalties" and "manual actions" with varying degrees of precision. Unlike several prior topics, however, this specific cluster turned out to be **substantially more accurate and directly traceable to Google's actual primary documentation** than the equivalent content encountered in Topics F and H — most of these sources' substantive procedural claims (the technical/quality split, the invisible-content rule, the completeness requirement, the manual-action mechanism) are independently confirmed, often near-verbatim, by Google's own official structured-data-policies page (fetched directly for this document, full text below). This is worth noting explicitly as a positive counterpoint to this series' recurring source-quality warnings: **not all SEO-adjacent commercial content is equally unreliable**, and where a claim is independently checkable against a primary source and confirmed, it should be credited at FACT tier via the primary source, not dismissed merely because of where it was first encountered. ## §1 — The technical/quality guideline split, and content-mismatch as the load-bearing mechanism

### A. What we need to understand
Google's structured-data documentation appears, on first read, to just be a checklist (required properties, supported formats, don't do X). Is there a more precise, more useful internal structure to these guidelines — specifically, a distinction between what a syntax validator can catch and what it fundamentally cannot — that should reshape how this hackathon's structured-data skill is designed?

### B. Why it matters for the hackathon
If such a split exists and is real (not an interpretive gloss this document is imposing), it directly identifies the single highest-value, least-obvious check this topic can propose: the category of defect that **passes automated syntax validation yet still constitutes a real, documented violation** is, by construction, invisible to any team that builds a schema checker using only an off-the-shelf JSON-LD/schema.org validator library — which is almost certainly what most competing hackathon teams will do, making this genuinely differentiating.

### C. Current evidence
- **FACT (Google, first-party, direct, the single most load-bearing citation in this document):** Google's own General Structured Data Guidelines page explicitly, formally divides its own requirements into two named categories with an explicit statement of their different testability: **"Technical guidelines"** ("You can test compliance with technical guidelines using the Rich Results Test and the URL Inspection tool, which catch most technical errors") covering format (JSON-LD/Microdata/RDFa) and access (not blocked by robots.txt/noindex) — versus **"Quality guidelines"**, for which Google's own text states directly and unambiguously: **"These quality guidelines are not easily testable using an automated tool. Violating a quality guideline can prevent syntactically correct structured data from being displayed as a rich result... or possibly cause it to be marked as spam."** This is not an interpretation or inference — it is Google's own explicit, stated categorization, in its own documentation's own section headers.
- **FACT (Google, first-party, direct, the specific content-mismatch mechanism):** under the Quality Guidelines' "Content" subsection, Google states directly: **"Don't mark up content that is not visible to readers of the page. For example, if the JSON-LD markup describes a performer, the HTML body must describe that same performer."** This is a precise, concrete, machine-checkable-in-principle rule (compare markup fields against visible page content) that sits specifically in the category Google itself says its own automated tools *cannot* catch — meaning a skill that performs this specific comparison is filling a real, self-declared gap in Google's own tooling, not merely duplicating what the Rich Results Test already does.
- **FACT (Google, first-party, direct, the "Relevance" mechanism, distinct from and complementary to content-mismatch):** the guidelines' "Relevance" subsection states "Your structured data must be a true representation of the page content," with concrete named examples: "A sports live streaming site labeling broadcasts as local events. A woodworking site labeling instructions as recipes." This is a **type-mismatch** mechanism (using the wrong schema.org *type* for what the page actually is) — distinct from, though related to, the content-mismatch mechanism above (which concerns *field values* within a correctly-typed markup block not matching visible content). Both are explicitly named quality-guideline failure modes not caught by automated syntax tools.
- **FACT (Google, first-party, direct, on manual-action consequences, confirming this is not a minor technicality):** Google states structured-data issues "can result in a manual action," which "means that a page loses eligibility for appearance as a rich result" — and, separately (cross-checked against the completeness subsection below), that fake/manipulated reviews specifically "may result in manual action." This confirms real, documented enforcement consequences exist for exactly the content-mismatch/relevance failure modes this unit identifies, not merely theoretical guideline violations.
- **FACT (Google, first-party, direct, on the completeness trap — a specific, non-obvious sub-case of content-mismatch worth flagging on its own):** Google's guidelines state explicitly, under "Additional tips": **"if you include multiple reviews, make sure that you include all of the reviews that are visible to people on the page. If a page didn't mark up all of the reviews on a page, this would be misleading for people who expect to see all of those reviews."** This is a genuinely non-obvious, precise rule: it is not merely "don't fabricate reviews" (the more commonly-discussed fake-review problem) but **"don't selectively mark up only some of your real, visible reviews"** — a subtler, easy-to-accidentally-violate pattern (e.g., a developer implementing review schema might reasonably, but incorrectly, only markup a "featured reviews" subset) that most teams building a simple presence/absence or fake-review checker would not think to test for.
- **INFERENCE, directly connecting this unit to the hackathon's stated research priorities:** the brief's own research-priority list explicitly names "false-positive reduction" and does not name "count how many schema types are present" — the content-mismatch/relevance mechanism this unit identifies is a genuinely different *kind* of check from a presence-counting one: it requires actually reading and comparing two things (markup field values, visible page text) rather than checking a box, and it directly explains a real, if under-discussed, root-cause chain worth stating explicitly: **stale/incorrect structured data can independently mislead an AI system even when the visible page content itself is accurate and current** — a system that trusts the markup over the rendered text (or vice versa, with no way to know which to prefer) receives a materially different signal than a system reading only the visible page, and Google's own explicit content-mismatch rule confirms this is a recognized, real-world failure pattern, not a hypothetical one invented for this hackathon.

### D. Important mechanisms
The technical/quality split is the organizing mechanism for this entire topic: **automated schema *validators* check syntax and required-field presence; they structurally cannot and do not check whether the markup's actual field values agree with what a human reader sees on the page.** This is a genuine, primary-source-confirmed gap between "passes validation" and "is actually correct and non-misleading" — directly parallel to and reinforcing the general pattern this document series has repeatedly found (Topic P's stale-vs-current distinction, Topic F's collision-risk-vs-mere-name-uniqueness distinction): **presence or syntactic correctness of a signal is not the same as the signal being trustworthy**, and the interesting, differentiating check is always in that gap, not in the presence check itself.

### E. Concrete website signals
For each page carrying structured data of a type in this document's scope (`Product`, `Offer`, `Review`/`AggregateRating`, `Article`, `Event`, `FAQPage`): extract the markup's key field values (price, availability, review count/rating, author, publish/modified date, event date/location) and compare each against the corresponding visible, rendered page content for the same fact. Separately: check whether the markup's declared `@type` plausibly matches the page's evident primary content type (per the Relevance mechanism's named examples). Separately again: for `Review`/`AggregateRating` specifically, check whether the count of individually-marked-up review items is consistent with the number of reviews visibly rendered on the page (the completeness-trap mechanism).

### F. How the signal could be detected automatically
Deterministic-first, directly reusing infrastructure this document series has already established: (1) parse JSON-LD/Microdata/RDFa blocks and extract field values per type; (2) extract corresponding visible-text values from the rendered DOM using the same fact-pattern extraction approach established in Pulkit's Topic A (A16/A17 — currency/date/number pattern extraction) and this document's own Topic P §2 (claim-value extraction); (3) run the shared comparison function already established in Topic P §4 (`compare_claim_against_source`), here applied with the markup itself as one "source" and the rendered page as the other, rather than an external website — a direct, elegant reuse of infrastructure this series has already built for a structurally identical comparison task; (4) for `@type`/Relevance mismatch, a lighter check: does the page's dominant visible content genre (product listing, article, event listing, recipe/instructions) match the declared schema type — escalate ambiguous cases to a single LLM judgment, consistent with this series' standing hybrid-approach discipline.

### G. What evidence the skill should report
For each checked structured-data block: the specific field(s) compared, the markup value, the corresponding visible-page value, and a match/mismatch verdict; for `@type`/Relevance checks, the declared type versus the page's apparent actual content genre; for Review/AggregateRating specifically, the marked-up review count versus the visibly-rendered review count.

### H. Possible severity logic
- **High:** a content-mismatch on a fact type with real downstream consequence (price, availability, review rating/count) — directly matching Google's own stated consequence (manual action, loss of rich-result eligibility) and this document series' own consistently-applied materiality-gating discipline (Topic P §2).
- **Medium:** `@type`/Relevance mismatch (wrong schema type for the page's evident content) — a real, named violation, but with less direct immediate factual-harm consequence than a value-level content mismatch.
- **Low:** minor/cosmetic field discrepancies (formatting differences, not material value differences — directly reusing Topic P §2's materiality-axis discipline to avoid over-flagging trivial variance).

### I. Correct remediation
Update the structured-data field values to match current visible page content (or vice versa, whichever is actually correct — directly reusing Topic P's directionality-reporting discipline rather than assuming the markup or the visible content is automatically the "true" one); ensure the declared `@type` matches the page's genuine primary content type; ensure all visibly-rendered review instances are represented in `Review`/`AggregateRating` markup, not a curated subset.

### J. False-positive cases
Minor, non-material formatting differences (a price shown as "$49.00" in markup and "$49" in visible text) are not a genuine content mismatch and must not be flagged at the same severity as a genuine value discrepancy — this requires the same materiality-aware comparison logic (not naive string-diffing) established in Topic P §2. A page legitimately using multiple nested/individual schema items for genuinely different but related content (per Google's own documented "multiple items on a page" pattern — a Recipe with nested Video and Review) is not a defect; this document's checks must correctly parse nested vs. individual item structures per Google's own documented conventions, not flag legitimate multi-item markup as some kind of inconsistency.

### K. False-negative risks
A sophisticated bad actor could deliberately keep markup and visible content in sync while both are simultaneously stale or wrong relative to *external* reality (e.g., markup and visible text both show an outdated price) — this unit's content-mismatch check only catches *internal* (markup-vs-page) disagreement, not *external* (page-vs-reality) staleness, which is Topic P's separate territory; the two checks are complementary, not redundant, and neither substitutes for the other.

### L. Counterexamples
A page's structured data intentionally including supplementary information not literally visible as rendered text but legitimately implied/summarized by visible content (e.g., a structured `description` field that's a reasonable paraphrase, not a verbatim quote, of visible marketing copy) should not be flagged as a mismatch — Google's own rule is about the *underlying fact* (who the performer is, what the price is) being visible, not requiring verbatim textual identity; this document's detection design must compare underlying values/facts, not exact string matches, consistent with how Topic P §2 and F41-F46 both already handle this same nuance.

### M. Does this generalize across site types?
Yes, universally — the technical/quality-guideline split and the specific content-mismatch mechanism are general schema.org/Google-documentation-level facts, not site-type-specific. The specific schema *types* most relevant to check (Product/Offer for e-commerce, Event for venues, Article for publishers, JobPosting for recruiters) naturally vary by site type — a direct, low-friction hook to Topic V, since the underlying mechanism and detection method are identical regardless of which type is being checked.

### N. Candidate skill(s)
**`structured-data-integrity-check`** — the primary detection skill for this unit, built around the shared `compare_claim_against_source` infrastructure already established in Topic P §4, applied here to a markup-vs-rendered-page comparison rather than a site-vs-external-source comparison.

### O. Relationship to other potential skills
Directly reuses Topic P §4's shared comparison infrastructure (a genuinely elegant, unplanned third use-case for that shared component, alongside Topic F's F37-F40 and Topic H's §4 — worth flagging to the team as further validation that building it once was the right call). Composes with Topic F's `entity-identity-audit` at the explicit boundary already established (Topic F owns `sameAs`/`@id`/entity-identity fields specifically; this unit owns general field-value/type correctness for all other schema types) — a page could plausibly generate findings from both skills simultaneously (e.g., a `LocalBusiness` page with both a stale `sameAs` link, per Topic F, and a mismatched `priceRange`, per this unit), which is a legitimate case for Topic T's aggregation logic to evaluate for a potential connected diagnosis, not an automatic assumption that they're related.

## §2 — Completeness, specificity, and multi-item linking as machine-comprehension mechanisms

### A. What we need to understand
Beyond the content-mismatch mechanism (§1), Google's documentation describes several more structural rules — required-vs-recommended property completeness, type specificity, and `@id`-based linking between multiple structured-data items on the same page. Are these genuinely separate, checkable mechanisms, or restatements of the same underlying "be thorough and accurate" principle already covered by §1?

### B. Why it matters
This determines whether Topic G needs a second distinct check beyond content-mismatch, or whether these items should simply be folded into §1 as minor sub-checks — a genuine question this document should answer explicitly rather than padding the skill with redundant checks, consistent with this series' standing discipline against manufactured findings.

### C. Current evidence
- **FACT (Google, first-party, direct, on required-property completeness — genuinely distinct from §1's content-mismatch mechanism):** "Specify all required properties listed in the documentation for your specific rich result type. Items that are missing required properties are not eligible for rich results." This is a straightforward, syntactically-checkable completeness rule — genuinely different in kind from §1's quality-guideline content-mismatch mechanism, since **this one *is* caught by the Rich Results Test** (it's explicitly a technical-guideline-adjacent completeness check, not a quality-guideline one), meaning it's a lower-novelty, lower-differentiation check (any competent validator already does this) but still worth including as a baseline, cheap, deterministic pre-check before the more valuable §1 comparison work.
- **FACT (Google, first-party, direct, on recommended-property completeness — a genuinely more interesting quality signal):** "The more recommended properties that you provide, the higher quality the result is to users... Rich result ranking takes extra information into consideration" — with concrete named examples (users prefer job postings with explicit salaries; users prefer recipes with genuine reviews/ratings). This is directly, explicitly a **graded completeness quality signal**, not a binary pass/fail — a page can be technically valid (all required properties present) yet still meaningfully thinner/lower-quality than a fuller implementation, a distinct and useful gradation this document's severity logic should represent rather than collapsing into a binary valid/invalid check.
- **FACT (Google, first-party, direct, on the `@id`-linking mechanism — a genuinely distinct, non-obvious structural mechanism):** "If there are items that are more helpful when they are linked together (for example, a recipe and a video), use `@id` in both the recipe and the video items to specify that the video is about the recipe on the page. If you didn't link the items together, Google Search may not know that it can show the video as a Recipe rich result." This is a precise, non-obvious, directly documented mechanism: **multiple, individually-valid structured-data items on the same page can fail to be correctly associated with each other if not explicitly `@id`-linked**, even though each item in isolation is perfectly valid — a genuine, distinct failure mode from both simple presence/absence and from §1's content-mismatch mechanism, and one a naive "does this page have valid schema" check would completely miss (each individual block would validate cleanly).
- **FACT (Google, first-party, direct, on specificity):** "Try to use the most specific applicable type and property names defined by schema.org for your markup" — a general best-practice statement, less independently checkable/novel than the completeness and `@id`-linking mechanisms above (it's closer to general guidance than a distinct, testable failure mode), and treated accordingly at lower priority in this unit's design.

### D. Important mechanisms
Two genuinely distinct, non-redundant mechanisms beyond §1: **(1) graded (not binary) completeness** — recommended-property richness is itself a quality signal Google explicitly says affects rich-result ranking consideration, meaning a "technically valid, minimally complete" page is meaningfully different from a "technically valid, richly complete" page even though both would pass a simple validator; and **(2) cross-item linking** — multiple valid structured-data blocks on one page can fail to be correctly understood as related without explicit `@id` connections, a structural failure invisible to any single-item validation pass.

### E. Concrete website signals
Ratio of recommended (not just required) properties populated, relative to the full documented property set for the page's declared schema type; presence of multiple structured-data items on a page that are contextually/visually related (e.g., a product and its reviews, a recipe and its demonstration video) but lack `@id` cross-references connecting them.

### F. How the signal could be detected automatically
Deterministic: parse all structured-data blocks on a page; for completeness, compare populated properties against a maintained reference list of required vs. recommended properties per schema.org type (a static, schema.org-documentation-derived lookup table, cheap and fully deterministic); for `@id`-linking, detect multiple distinct valid items on the same page whose content is evidently related (via simple heuristics — shared proper nouns, visual/DOM proximity) but which lack `@id` cross-references, escalating genuinely ambiguous "are these actually related" judgments to a single LLM check.

### G. What evidence the skill should report
Required-property completeness (binary pass/fail, cheap baseline); recommended-property completeness ratio (graded); any detected pairs of evidently-related items lacking `@id` linkage, with a brief explanation of what rich-result opportunity is likely being missed as a result.

### H. Possible severity logic
- **Medium-High:** missing required properties (excludes the page from rich-result eligibility entirely — a real, if narrowly-scoped, consequence).
- **Low, framed as proactive recommendation rather than defect:** low recommended-property completeness — directly matching this document series' standing practice (Topic F's F-02, Topic H's §1) of treating "could be richer" findings as suggestions, not defects, since Google's own language ("higher quality... to users") frames this as a quality gradient, not a violation.
- **Low-Medium:** missing `@id` linkage between evidently-related items — a real, documented missed opportunity, but not itself a violation of any guideline (distinct in kind from §1's quality-guideline violations).

### I. Correct remediation
Add missing required properties first (cheapest, highest-eligibility-impact fix); progressively add recommended properties where genuinely available (not fabricated — this must be stated explicitly to avoid inadvertently encouraging the exact "recommended properties with less complete, badly-formed, or inaccurate data" anti-pattern Google's own Organization documentation, referenced in Topic F, explicitly warns against); add `@id` cross-references between evidently-related structured-data items already present on the page.

### J. False-positive cases
Not every schema.org-defined recommended property is relevant or available for every specific page's actual content — Google's own Organization-documentation language (already established in Topic F) explicitly prefers fewer accurate properties over more incomplete/inaccurate ones; this unit's recommended-completeness scoring must never imply that maximizing property count is itself the goal, and should be framed strictly as "these commonly-relevant properties appear absent, consider whether they apply," not as a hard target to fill.

### K. False-negative risks
The static required/recommended property reference table needs periodic maintenance as schema.org and Google's own per-type documentation evolve — a stale internal reference table could miss newly-required properties or flag deprecated ones as still relevant (directly connecting to the "Deprecated types aren't errors" observation from the source-quality note in §1 — FAQPage and HowTo rich-result eligibility changes are a real, live example of exactly this kind of drift risk, requiring this table to be built with an explicit update/maintenance path in mind, not treated as a one-time static asset).

### L. Counterexamples
A page with only required properties populated, by deliberate minimalist design choice, and no genuinely applicable recommended properties to add (e.g., a very simple local-business listing with no meaningful additional detail to offer) is not a defect — the low-severity, proactive-suggestion framing (§H) is specifically designed to avoid over-penalizing this legitimate case.

### M. Does this generalize across site types?
Yes — the completeness-gradation and `@id`-linking mechanisms are general schema.org/Google-documentation facts; which specific properties are commonly relevant varies by schema type and therefore by site type (Topic V hook, consistent with §1).

### N. Candidate skill(s)
Folds into **`structured-data-integrity-check`** as two additional sub-checks (graded completeness, `@id`-linkage detection) alongside §1's primary content-mismatch check — not separate skills, since all three operate on the same underlying parsed structured-data extraction and share the same skill folder's natural scope.

### O. Relationship to other potential skills
The required-property completeness sub-check is the lowest-novelty, most "any competent validator already does this" component of the whole topic — included as a cheap deterministic baseline, not the differentiating contribution. The `@id`-linkage and graded-completeness checks are the genuinely distinctive additions this unit contributes beyond what a generic schema validator (which most competing teams will likely just wrap) would surface.

**Observation:** Google's documentation explicitly distinguishes required-property completeness (binary, syntactically checkable, excludes a page from rich-result eligibility if violated) from recommended-property completeness (an explicitly graded quality signal Google states affects rich-result ranking consideration, not a pass/fail rule) and separately documents a precise `@id`-based cross-item-linking mechanism, explicitly stating that without it, "Google Search may not know" that multiple individually-valid structured-data items on the same page are related.
**Evidence:** Google Search Central, "General structured data guidelines" (Completeness and Multiple items on a page / Nesting sections), developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/sd-policies.
**Sources:** See §2 §C.
**Pattern:** Three genuinely distinct structured-data failure mechanisms exist beyond simple presence/absence: content-mismatch (§1, quality-guideline, high-novelty), completeness gradation (this finding, technical-guideline-adjacent for the required-property case, low-novelty; quality-signal for the recommended-property case, medium-novelty), and cross-item linking (this finding, structurally distinct, high-novelty since it's invisible to any single-item validation pass).
**Counterexamples:** Maximizing recommended-property count is not itself the goal — Google's own Organization-documentation language (established in Topic F) explicitly prefers fewer accurate properties over more incomplete/inaccurate ones; a page with only required properties by deliberate minimalist design, with no genuinely applicable additional properties, is not a defect.
**Hypothesis:** A dedicated `@id`-linkage detection check (evidently-related structured-data items on the same page lacking cross-references) will surface real missed rich-result opportunities invisible to standard single-item schema validation, at meaningfully lower false-positive risk than a naive completeness-maximization check would carry.
**Signal:** Populated-vs-documented recommended-property ratio per schema type; presence of multiple evidently-related structured-data items on a page lacking `@id` cross-references.
**How to Detect:** Deterministic property-population comparison against a maintained required/recommended reference table per type; heuristic relatedness detection (shared proper nouns, DOM proximity) for `@id`-linkage candidates, with LLM escalation for ambiguous relatedness judgments.
**Evidence Output:** Required-property completeness (binary); recommended-property completeness ratio (graded, framed as suggestion not defect); detected unlinked-but-related item pairs with an explanation of the likely missed rich-result opportunity.
**False Positives:** Recommended-property scoring must never imply fabricating data to fill properties is the goal; low-relevance properties for a given page's actual content should not be penalized.
**False Negatives:** The required/recommended reference table requires ongoing maintenance as schema.org and Google's per-type documentation evolve (concretely illustrated by the FAQPage/HowTo rich-result deprecations noted in this document's source-quality discussion) — a stale table risks false guidance in either direction.
**Severity:** Medium-High (missing required properties) to Low (recommended-property gaps, framed as suggestion) to Low-Medium (`@id`-linkage gaps).
**Recommended Fix:** Add missing required properties first; add genuinely-applicable recommended properties without fabrication; add `@id` cross-references between already-present, evidently-related structured-data items.
**Generalization:** High — general schema.org/Google-documentation mechanisms; specific relevant properties vary by schema type/site type (Topic V hook).
**Candidate Skill:** Folds into `structured-data-integrity-check` alongside Finding G-01's primary check, not a standalone skill.
**Related Skills:** Same skill folder as G-01; required-property completeness is the lowest-novelty component (any competent off-the-shelf validator already does this) and is included as a cheap deterministic baseline rather than the topic's differentiating contribution.
**Confidence:** HIGH — based on the same directly-fetched, first-party primary source as G-01.

## 3. Required end-of-topic synthesis

**Strongest validated insight:**
Google's own structured-data documentation explicitly, formally distinguishes what its own automated tools *can* catch (technical guidelines — format, access, required-property presence) from what they explicitly *cannot* (quality guidelines — content-mismatch, type/Relevance mismatch, review-completeness) — a directly-sourced, first-party confirmation that the highest-value contribution this topic can make is not "check if schema exists" or "validate JSON-LD syntax" (which any off-the-shelf validator library already does, and which most competing hackathon teams will likely just wrap), but specifically the markup-vs-visible-content comparison Google itself says its own tooling cannot perform. This is among the cleanest, most directly-sourced findings in this entire document series — it rests on a single, explicit, unambiguous primary-source statement rather than requiring synthesis across multiple weaker sources.

**Strongest unvalidated hypothesis:**
That the `@id`-based cross-item-linking check (§2, Finding G-02) will surface a meaningful number of real findings in practice — this mechanism is clearly, explicitly documented by Google as real and consequential, but this document has no data on how commonly sites actually have multiple evidently-related-but-unlinked structured-data items (as opposed to either fully separate unrelated items, correctly, or fully linked items, correctly) — an empirical question this document flags but cannot answer from documentation research alone.

**Strongest candidate skill:**
`structured-data-integrity-check`, built primarily around Finding G-01's content-mismatch/Relevance mechanism (the clearest, most directly-sourced, most genuinely differentiating check in this document) with Finding G-02's completeness-gradation and `@id`-linkage checks as secondary, lower-priority-but-still-valuable additions to the same skill — and notably, the third independent reuse of the shared `compare_claim_against_source` infrastructure established in Topic P §4, now validated across three genuinely different application contexts (external-KG comparison in Topic F, cross-source corroboration in Topic H, and markup-vs-rendered-page comparison here), which is itself a meaningful confirmation that building it once as shared infrastructure was the right design decision.

**Weakest assumption we should investigate next:**
This document assumes the rendered-DOM fact-extraction approach already established in Pulkit's Topic A (A16/A17) will transfer cleanly to extracting comparable values for the structured-data-vs-page comparison — but that extraction approach was designed and evidenced for a different original purpose (assessing chunk/passage self-containment for AI retrieval, not specifically for markup-comparison). Before finalizing `structured-data-integrity-check`'s detection logic, this reuse should be explicitly validated against a handful of real pages with known, deliberately-introduced markup/content mismatches, rather than assumed to work identically in this new application context.
