## 0. Framing — what Topic M is actually for

Topic M is not "good UX practices" as trivia. It exists to answer one design question for the marketplace:

> **When a visitor arrives at a website — specifically a visitor referred by an AI assistant's citation, who arrives carrying an AI-generated paraphrase in their head rather than pre-existing brand familiarity — can a read-only audit detect, with real evidentiary grounding rather than generic UX-checklist intuition, whether the landing experience will fail to establish credibility, clarity, or navigability within the narrow window research shows actually matters?**

Every sub-topic below is evaluated against that question, not against "is this interesting UX trivia."

The master research map lists M1–M55 under this topic — the largest raw list of any topic in this series. Following the same condense-and-focus discipline used in Topics F, H, and P, this document organizes M1–M55 into **three genuinely distinct investigation units**, because close reading shows the raw list is overwhelmingly elaboration on two well-evidenced psychological mechanisms plus one distinctly different, AI-era-specific concern:

1. **§1 — First-impression credibility formation**: what does the actual peer-reviewed HCI/credibility literature say about *how fast* and *on what basis* visitors judge a site, and is this genuinely fast enough to matter for a structural, automatable check (M1–M2, M11, M20–M22, M27–M28, M35, M54, and the credibility-adjacent items throughout)?
2. **§2 — Information scent and navigability**: is "can a visitor predict what a link leads to" a real, formal, measurable theory — not just intuition — and can it be operationalized deterministically (M6, M23–M26, M29–M30, M45–M49)?
3. **§3 — AI-referral-specific landing-page match**: does the *referral source* (AI-assistant citation vs. organic search vs. direct navigation) change what "good landing experience" means, in a way distinct from general UX best practice, and is this the genuinely novel contribution Topic M should make beyond restating established web-usability research (M16–M22, connecting directly to the master list's separate Topic X, "AI-to-Human Handoff")?

The remaining items (M3–M5, M7–M10, M12–M15, M17–M19, M31–M34, M36–M44, M50–M53, M55) are either direct sub-cases of these three mechanisms (folded into the relevant unit below) or fall squarely into adjacent topics already owned by teammates (page speed/Core Web Vitals → Topic O; accessibility/WCAG → Topic N; form design/conversion friction → outside this hackathon's stated scope of AI discoverability and engagement diagnosis, not itself an AI-or-search-driven failure mode) and are not duplicated here.

**Constraint from the handout, re-read carefully:** the handout frames the full chain as *reachable → readable → extractable → cited → landed → understood → trusted → engaged*. Topic M owns the back half of that chain specifically for the case where the visitor arrived via an AI citation, not organic search or a bookmark — the referral context is the load-bearing distinction between "just do good UX" and this topic's actual, narrower brief.

## 1. FACT vs OBSERVATION vs HYPOTHESIS vs INFERENCE vs SPECULATION — global legend

Same legend as Topics F, H, and P, used identically here:
- **FACT** — documented by the vendor/standards body, or established in peer-reviewed/archival literature.
- **OBSERVATION** — reported by independent researchers/practitioners running real, methodologically transparent trials (not vendor-confirmed).
- **HYPOTHESIS** — our proposed testable claim, not yet validated by us.
- **INFERENCE** — reasonable derivation from FACT/OBSERVATION, one logical step removed.
- **SPECULATION** — plausible but unconfirmed; flagged, not built on.

**A note on source quality control, continuing the pattern established in Topics F, H, and P.** This topic's research surfaced the clearest split yet between reliable and unreliable sources in this entire document series. On one side: a genuinely strong, decades-deep, peer-reviewed HCI literature — Lindgaard, Fernandes, Dudek & Brown's foundational 50-millisecond first-impression study (published in *Behaviour & Information Technology*, 2006), Robins & Holmes' 3.42-second credibility-assessment-stability finding, Fogg et al.'s Prominence-Interpretation Theory and Stanford Web Credibility Research program, a controlled aesthetics-credibility study demonstrating an "amelioration effect," and Pirolli & Card's Information Foraging Theory (*Psychological Review*, 1999) with its own line of automated-evaluation tooling (BLOODHOUND, the Cognitive Walkthrough for the Web). On the other side: a large cluster of 2025–2026 marketing/web-design-agency blog posts (Midas Touch Infotech, Made For Web, Aspiration Marketing, SAMPS) that all cite the *same* handful of headline statistics — "75% of users judge credibility by design (Stanford)," "94% of first impressions are design-related (Northumbria University)," "38% will leave if unattractive (Adobe)" — with **no consistent, traceable original source**, no methodology, and phrasing similar enough across nominally unrelated sites to suggest derivative copying rather than independent citation of a real study. This is the same F-00/H-04 pattern in a new domain. **I am not building any detection threshold on these specific percentages.** Where this document cites first-impression speed or credibility findings, it cites the traceable peer-reviewed originals (Lindgaard et al.'s actual 50ms finding, Robins & Holmes' actual 3.42-second finding) rather than the marketing-blog restatements of them, and treats the widely-circulated round percentages as unverifiable.

## §1 — First-impression credibility formation

### A. What we need to understand
Is "visitors judge a site almost instantly" a real, peer-reviewed, quantified finding — and if so, precisely how fast, on what basis, and how stable is that initial judgment — in enough mechanistic detail to justify treating "first-viewport credibility" as a distinct, structurally-checkable audit target rather than a vague UX platitude?

### B. Why it matters for the hackathon
If the credibility judgment genuinely forms and stabilizes within a few seconds (or less), then a static, single-snapshot crawl-based audit — which is exactly what this hackathon's runtime constraint allows — is actually well-matched to the phenomenon: a fast, largely non-analytical, gestalt-level judgment is far more plausibly approximated by structural/heuristic analysis of a page's above-the-fold state than a slower, deliberative, content-reading process would be. This is a genuinely important methodological validation for the whole topic, not just supporting color.

### C. Current evidence
- **FACT (peer-reviewed, foundational, directly on point):** Lindgaard, Fernandes, Dudek & Brown, "Attention web designers: You have 50 milliseconds to make a good first impression!" (*Behaviour & Information Technology*, 2006) is the original, widely-replicated study establishing that visual appeal judgments of web pages form within as little as **50 milliseconds** of exposure — far too fast for deliberate, analytical evaluation of content, meaning the initial judgment is a rapid, holistic, largely pre-cognitive visual-gestalt response, not a reasoned assessment of the page's actual informational quality.
- **FACT (peer-reviewed, directly on point, a second independent finding on judgment *stability*, not just speed):** Robins & Holmes (2008) found that users' credibility assessments of a webpage become **stable after approximately 3.42 seconds** of exposure — additional viewing time beyond that point had little further effect on changing the assessment. This is a distinct and important complement to the Lindgaard finding: the *initial* aesthetic reaction forms in milliseconds, but the *credibility* judgment specifically (a related but distinct construct) stabilizes on the order of a few seconds — both figures converge on the same practical conclusion (judgment happens fast and then locks in), but it is worth being precise that they measure different constructs (visual appeal vs. credibility) at different timescales, rather than treating them as the same finding restated.
- **FACT (peer-reviewed, directly on point, a controlled experimental demonstration of *why* this matters beyond a lab curiosity):** Fogg et al.'s Stanford Web Credibility Research program developed **Prominence-Interpretation Theory**, a formal, tested model stating that a credibility judgment requires both that a user *notices* an element (prominence) and *makes some interpretation* about it (interpretation) — meaning credibility signals that are structurally present but not prominent (buried, small, low-contrast) fail to register at all, regardless of their actual informational value. This is a genuinely useful, mechanism-level, peer-reviewed model — not just "make things look nice" — because it gives a concrete two-part test (is a signal prominent enough to be noticed; would a reasonable visitor interpret it favorably) that maps directly onto a structural, automatable audit.
- **FACT (peer-reviewed, controlled experimental study, directly demonstrating a real causal mechanism rather than mere correlation):** a controlled study (published in a peer-reviewed HCI venue, widely cited as "the amelioration effect") presented **identical content** at different levels of aesthetic/visual treatment and found the more aesthetically-treated version was judged as **more credible**, despite the underlying informational content being the same. This is a rare case in this research area of a study design that actually isolates the causal variable (content held constant, only visual treatment varied) — directly supporting that visual/structural presentation genuinely *causes* credibility perception shifts, independent of content quality, rather than merely correlating with it.
- **FACT (peer-reviewed, directly relevant to a specific, checkable structural pattern — premature conversion asks):** Nielsen Norman Group's synthesis of first-impression research (itself citing the automaticity/dual-process literature on fast, non-conscious cognitive processing) documents a specific, concrete anti-pattern directly relevant to structural detection: a homepage that leads with an aggressive, high-commitment call-to-action (e.g., a prominent registration/contact-information demand as the first thing a visitor sees, before any value has been demonstrated) is empirically associated with visitors perceiving the site as "needy" and becoming "annoyed and suspicious" — an erosion of exactly the credibility judgment described above, triggered by a structurally identifiable pattern (primary CTA prominence and type relative to value-proposition prominence).
- **OBSERVATION, explicitly downgraded (source-quality note applies in full):** the widely-circulated "75%/94%/38%" statistics cluster (§1 note) is not used as evidentiary support anywhere in this document, despite being the single most repeated claim across every source encountered in this topic's research — a deliberate, explicit choice consistent with this document series' standing discipline of preferring the traceable peer-reviewed original over its derivative restatement, even when the derivative is far more prevalent in search results.

### D. Important mechanisms
Two compounding, evidenced mechanisms: **(1) the credibility judgment is fast, holistic, and largely non-analytical** (Lindgaard's 50ms finding; Robins & Holmes' 3.42-second stabilization) — meaning it is driven by structural/visual gestalt properties detectable by a static crawl, not by a visitor's careful reading of prose content; and **(2) a credibility signal that exists but isn't prominent enough to be noticed functions identically to a signal that doesn't exist at all** (Prominence-Interpretation Theory) — meaning "the information is technically present somewhere on the page" is not sufficient; *where* and *how visually prominent* it is within the fast-judgment window is the actual mechanism. This directly parallels the "context-insufficiency" mechanism established across Topics F, H, and P (an AI system relying on ambiguous context fails in a specific, evidenced way) — here, the human visitor's fast, largely automatic first-impression system is the analogous "reader" whose failure mode this document needs to model.

### E. Concrete website signals
Presence, above-the-fold visibility, and structural prominence (heading level, position, visual weight proxies like whether it's in a hero/header region versus buried in a footer) of: a clear value-proposition statement, an identifiable primary call-to-action, and basic credibility-signaling elements (contact/about information, not necessarily elaborate — per Prominence-Interpretation Theory, presence-without-prominence is equivalent to absence). Also: the specific anti-pattern identified in §C — an aggressive, high-commitment CTA (e.g., "Sign up now," a mandatory contact-info gate) positioned as the single most prominent above-the-fold element, with no value-establishing content preceding or surrounding it.

### F. How the signal could be detected automatically
Deterministic-first: parse the rendered DOM's above-the-fold region (using standard viewport-height heuristics); extract heading hierarchy, hero-region text content, and interactive-element (button/form/link) types and positions within that region. Classify whether a clear, specific value-proposition sentence exists in the hero region (reusing the specificity-detection approach established in Pulkit's Topic A findings on explicit, unhedged factual statements) and whether the most visually prominent interactive element is a low-commitment action (e.g., "Learn more," "Browse," continued scrolling) versus a high-commitment one (mandatory signup/contact gate) with no preceding value content. Escalate to a single LLM semantic-clarity check only for genuinely ambiguous value-proposition wording, consistent with this document series' standing hybrid-approach discipline.

### G. What evidence the skill should report
Whether a specific, non-generic value-proposition statement exists in the above-the-fold region (quoted, with position); whether basic credibility-signaling elements are present and prominent versus present-but-buried (an explicit Prominence-Interpretation-style two-part verdict, not a simple presence/absence check); whether the primary above-the-fold interactive element is high- or low-commitment, and whether it's preceded by any value-establishing content.

### H. Possible severity logic
- **High:** no discernible value-proposition statement in the above-the-fold region at all (a visitor arriving with an AI-generated paraphrase in mind, per §3, has no on-page content to confirm or orient against).
- **Medium:** value proposition present but generic/non-specific (marketing language with no concrete claim — directly reusing the claim-specificity distinction established across Pulkit's Topic A and this document series' own materiality work in Topic P §2), or a high-commitment CTA positioned with no preceding value content (the documented "needy/suspicious" anti-pattern).
- **Low:** minor prominence issues (a credibility signal present but positioned below an otherwise-reasonable fold).

### I. Correct remediation
Ensure a specific, concrete (not generic) value-proposition statement is present and visually prominent within the above-the-fold region; ensure the most prominent above-the-fold interactive element is a low-commitment action unless the page has already established sufficient value to justify a higher-commitment ask; ensure basic credibility signals (what the entity is, what it does, some minimal trust indicator) are not merely present in the HTML but structurally prominent.

### J. False-positive cases
A returning-user product dashboard, an internal tool, or any page where the visitor is known/assumed to already have context (e.g., a logged-in application state) legitimately doesn't need to re-establish a cold-visitor value proposition — this check should be scoped to genuinely public-facing, first-visit-oriented landing surfaces (homepage, top-level marketing/product pages), not applied blanket across an entire site including authenticated or deep-navigation pages. A minimal, deliberately sparse design (common for premium/luxury brands or well-known companies relying on brand recognition rather than explanation) is not automatically a defect — this connects directly to the referral-context distinction developed fully in §3: a visitor who already recognizes the brand needs less on-page orientation than one arriving cold via an unfamiliar AI citation.

### K. False-negative risks
Structural/DOM-based above-the-fold detection is a heuristic approximation of actual viewport rendering across different devices/screen sizes — a value proposition that's above-the-fold on a large desktop viewport might not be on a narrow mobile viewport, and a single-viewport-assumption check could miss this; the practical implementation should test against at least a common mobile and desktop viewport width, not just one.

### L. Counterexamples
The "amelioration effect" study (§C) demonstrating that identical content is judged more credible with better visual treatment cuts both ways as a limitation to state honestly: this document's structural/content-presence checks cannot directly measure aesthetic quality itself (color, typography, whitespace, professional polish) — only the *presence, positioning, and specificity* of the content that would need to be aesthetically well-presented. A page could pass every structural check in this unit and still fail the actual credibility judgment on pure visual-design-quality grounds this document's methodology cannot assess. This is an honest, explicitly-stated scope limitation, not a gap to paper over.

### M. Does this generalize across site types?
The underlying psychological mechanisms (fast, holistic judgment; prominence-gated credibility signaling) are general, peer-reviewed, human-cognition findings, not site-type-specific, so they generalize universally. The specific expectation for *what* the above-the-fold content should contain varies by site type and by how well-known the brand already is (per the false-positive guardrail above) — a direct hook to Topic V.

### N. Candidate skill(s)
**`first-viewport-orientation-check`** — the primary detection skill for this unit, combining deterministic structural extraction with the Prominence-Interpretation-style two-part (presence × prominence) verdict logic.

### O. Relationship to other potential skills
Directly parallels the "context-insufficiency" mechanism established across Topics F (entity self-disambiguation), H (corroboration/provenance), and P (temporal-currency legibility) — in each case, a documented reader (AI system or human visitor) fails when a signal exists but isn't structurally legible/prominent enough to register within that reader's actual processing constraints. Composes directly with §3 below (referral-context-specific severity weighting) and with Topic N (accessibility — the master list's own note that semantic HTML/heading structure serves both accessibility and this unit's prominence-detection mechanism simultaneously).

## §2 — Information scent and navigability

### A. What we need to understand
Is "can a visitor predict what a link/nav-item leads to before clicking it" a real, formal, mathematically-modeled theory with an established measurement approach — or is it merely a plausible-sounding UX intuition being retrofitted with academic-sounding language?

### B. Why it matters
This determines whether a navigation-quality check can be built on genuine theoretical grounding (with an actual, computable underlying model) versus an ad hoc "check if labels seem clear" heuristic — and, per the brief's own emphasis on mechanism-soundness over surface plausibility, this distinction matters for how much confidence the resulting skill's findings should carry.

### C. Current evidence
- **FACT (peer-reviewed, foundational, genuinely well-established — not a retrofitted intuition):** Information Foraging Theory (Pirolli & Card, *Psychological Review*, 1999, building on Pirolli's earlier 1995/1996 CHI papers) is a real, mathematically-formalized theory directly adapted from optimal foraging theory in behavioral ecology (Charnov's 1976 marginal value theorem — the mathematics animals use to decide when to abandon a depleting food patch for a new one). The theory models web navigation as a cost-benefit foraging process: users estimate the expected informational value of following a link based on **"information scent"** — proximal cues (link text, surrounding words, headings) that predict the value of the destination — and use a **spreading-activation mechanism** linking these cues to internal goals to decide where to click. This is not a metaphor loosely borrowed for rhetorical effect; it is a genuine, quantitatively-modeled cognitive theory with peer-reviewed mathematical grounding, independently developed and refined across multiple decades of publication (Card, Pirolli, Van Der Wege et al.'s 2001 CHI paper; Pirolli's 2006 evaluation-methods paper).
- **FACT (peer-reviewed, directly confirming this theory is operationalizable, not merely descriptive):** the theory has produced actual, working **automated usability evaluation tools** built directly on its mathematical model — notably **BLOODHOUND** (Chi, Rosien, Suppattanasiri, Williams, Royer, Chow et al., CHI 2003), which automates discovery of web usability issues by simulating foraging behavior using the InfoScent simulator, and the **Cognitive Walkthrough for the Web (CWW)** (Blackmon, Polson, Kitajima, Lewis), independently found to be superior for evaluating how well websites support navigation and information-search tasks. This is directly, materially useful for this hackathon: it is primary-source confirmation that automated, structural evaluation of information scent is a **solved, precedented category of tool**, not a novel or speculative undertaking — this document's proposed navigation-scent check has direct academic precedent, reducing implementation risk and validating the approach's feasibility within the hackathon's constraints.
- **FACT (peer-reviewed, mechanism detail, directly useful for detection design):** the operational unit of information scent is the **"trigger word"** — a term in a hyperlink's text, a heading, or surrounding context that a user perceives as meaningful to their current task; the theory holds that "the more trigger words that are present, the stronger the information scent" (a direct, quotable summary consistent with Pirolli's own spreading-activation formalization) — meaning the actual computable signal is a semantic-overlap measure between what the link/nav-label text says and what a plausible visitor's goal/query would contain, not a subjective "is this label clear" judgment.
- **INFERENCE:** because information scent's own predictive currency is trigger-word/semantic overlap between an anchor's surface text and the user's goal, a directly analogous, deterministically computable proxy is: **semantic similarity between a nav item/link's anchor text and its destination page's actual title/heading/summary content** — a link whose anchor text bears little resemblance to what the destination page is actually about is, per the theory's own mechanism, weak-scent by construction, independent of any subjective judgment about whether the label "sounds nice." This directly matches and reinforces (rather than duplicates) Pulkit's own Topic A A16 finding (heading-to-content topical alignment as a chunk-retrieval-quality signal) — the same underlying "does the label predict the content" mechanism, here applied to human navigation prediction rather than machine chunk-retrieval, a genuinely elegant convergence between an AI-mechanism finding and a human-cognition finding arrived at independently in two different topic documents.

### D. Important mechanisms
Information scent is a genuinely dual-purpose mechanism for this marketplace's stated goal of explaining *both* AI discoverability and human engagement failures from one connected diagnosis: the same underlying property (does surface text — a link label, a heading — accurately and specifically predict the content it points to) is evidenced, via two entirely independent research traditions, to matter for **both** human wayfinding (Pirolli & Card, this unit) **and** machine chunk-retrieval/heading-alignment (Pulkit's A16, and by extension Topic E). This is not a coincidence to gloss over — it is one of the strongest candidates in the entire research effort for a **single check whose finding genuinely serves both halves of the hackathon's stated objective** (why isn't it found/cited correctly, and why doesn't a human who arrives engage), directly matching the brief's explicit preference for connected, non-duplicative diagnosis over parallel unrelated findings.

### E. Concrete website signals
For each primary navigation element (main nav, footer nav, and prominent in-content CTAs — not every incidental in-body link, which the theory itself treats as lower-stakes/more associative): semantic overlap between the anchor text and the destination page's title/H1/lead-paragraph content.

### F. How the signal could be detected automatically
Deterministic-first: extract anchor text and destination-page title/H1/summary for all primary navigation elements (sampled once per unique template/nav pattern, reusing the template-clustering infrastructure Pulkit's Topic A A22 and Soham's Topic AF both establish as shared crawl infrastructure — not re-crawled per page); compute a straightforward lexical/keyword-overlap score as a first pass; escalate low-scoring or borderline pairs to a single LLM semantic-similarity judgment (a legitimate hybrid-approach use per the brief's guidance, since "does this label predict this content" is a genuinely semantic judgment beyond simple keyword matching in ambiguous cases).

### G. What evidence the skill should report
The specific nav/link anchor text, its destination, the computed overlap/alignment score, and — for flagged low-scent cases — a concrete example of what content the visitor would expect versus what they'd actually find.

### H. Possible severity logic
- **Medium-High:** primary/main navigation items (high-traffic, high-consequence for wayfinding) with low semantic alignment to their destination — directly evidenced by the theory to produce foraging failure (the visitor either doesn't click, or clicks and is disoriented/backtracks, per Pirolli's site-leaving model, which frames abandonment as an ongoing cost-benefit reassessment).
- **Low:** in-content, incidental links with weak alignment — the theory itself treats these as lower-stakes and more associative by nature, and flagging every loosely-associated in-body link would reproduce exactly the "big checklist" over-flagging problem this document series consistently guards against.

### I. Correct remediation
Rewrite navigation labels to use specific, concrete "trigger words" that genuinely match the destination content's actual topic — directly reusing the same "specific over generic" principle established across multiple prior documents in this series (Topic F's disambiguating prose, Pulkit's A17 quotable/specific-claim finding), here applied to navigation labeling specifically.

### J. False-positive cases
Deliberately playful, brand-voice-driven navigation labels (a distinctive but slightly oblique nav label that's part of an intentional brand personality) are not automatically a defect if the surrounding context (icon, position, or a brief accompanying description) still supplies adequate scent through channels beyond the label text alone — the check should assess overall predictive signal available at the point of decision, not treat literal label-text keyword matching as the only valid signal, and should be conservative about flagging stylistic choices absent clear evidence of genuine wayfinding failure.

### K. False-negative risks
A deterministic keyword-overlap first pass will miss genuine synonymy (a nav label "Solutions" leading to a page about "Products" might be semantically fine but lexically non-overlapping) — the LLM-escalation step is specifically there to catch this, but within the runtime budget, only borderline-scoring pairs can realistically be escalated, meaning some genuine synonym-based false positives from the deterministic pass may go uncorrected if the budget is exhausted.

### L. Counterexamples
Intentionally curiosity-driven or "mystery" navigation (a deliberate design pattern in some editorial/creative contexts, where withholding full predictive information is part of the intended experience — e.g., an arts/media site's intentionally evocative section names) is a legitimate design choice in specific site-type contexts, not a universal defect — reinforcing why this check's severity should be conditioned on site type/genre rather than applied as a flat rule.

### M. Does this generalize across site types?
The underlying theory and its mechanism are general, peer-reviewed, human-cognition findings that apply universally; the specific expectation for how literal/explicit versus evocative navigation labeling should be varies meaningfully by site type and genre (a Topic V hook, and here more genuinely context-dependent than in most other checks in this document series, per the counterexample above).

### N. Candidate skill(s)
**`navigation-scent-check`** — directly matching the name Pulkit's own Topic A document (A16 relationship notes) anticipated, template-level (sampled once per unique nav pattern, not per page, per the shared crawl-infrastructure design established across this document series).

### O. Relationship to other potential skills
This is the clearest example in the entire research effort of a single, well-evidenced mechanism serving both halves of the hackathon's mandate simultaneously — directly connects to Pulkit's Topic A A16 (heading-to-content alignment for machine chunk-retrieval) as the human-cognition mirror of the same underlying principle, and should very plausibly share detection infrastructure with it (both are fundamentally "does this label predict this content" checks, differing only in whether the "reader" is a human forager or a retrieval system) — a strong candidate for explicit co-design with Pulkit in the Combine phase, flagged accordingly below.

## §3 — AI-referral-specific landing-page match

### A. What we need to understand
Does the *specific circumstance* of arriving via an AI assistant's citation — as opposed to organic search, a bookmark, or word-of-mouth — change what "a good landing experience" requires, in a way that is genuinely distinct from (not merely a restatement of) the general web-usability findings in §1 and §2? This is the question that determines whether Topic M makes a genuinely novel contribution to the hackathon's specific AI-era framing, or merely re-packages decades-old usability research without adapting it to the actual brief.

### B. Why it matters for the hackathon
The handout explicitly frames the challenge around *both* halves — AI discoverability and on-site engagement — as one connected continuum, with the implicit premise that the specific referral mechanism (an AI paraphrase, not a search snippet or direct brand recall) changes the visitor's starting context in ways worth diagnosing specifically. If this document only restates §1/§2's general findings without addressing this distinction, it would fail to make Topic M's most hackathon-relevant contribution.

### C. Current evidence
- **INFERENCE, built by combining this document's own §1/§2 findings with Pulkit's Topic A findings on documented AI-assistant pipeline mechanics (no single external source directly studies "AI-referral landing page psychology" as its own field — this is a genuine, honestly-flagged evidence gap, addressed by principled synthesis rather than invented statistics):** a visitor referred by an AI assistant's citation arrives having already received a **paraphrased, synthesized answer** about the brand (per the documented retrieve→rank→generate→cite pipeline established across Pulkit's Topic A) — they are not arriving cold with zero context (unlike, arguably, some direct-navigation cases), but they are also not arriving with the search-engine-native context of having seen the site's *own* meta-description/title-tag snippet, the way an organic-search referral would. Instead, their pre-formed expectation is shaped by whatever the AI assistant's synthesis emphasized — which (per this document series' own Topic H and Topic F findings on volume/popularity bias and potential entity-collision risk) may or may not accurately reflect what the site itself would have emphasized about itself.
- **HYPOTHESIS (ours), directly combining §1's Prominence-Interpretation mechanism with this referral-context distinction:** an AI-referred visitor's fast (per §1's 50ms/3.42s findings), largely automatic first-impression judgment is specifically checking for **confirmation** — does what I'm now looking at match/support what I was just told — rather than forming a judgment from a completely blank slate the way a first-time, no-prior-context visitor would. This means the above-the-fold content's job for this specific visitor type is not merely "establish clarity and credibility in the abstract" (§1's general finding) but specifically "quickly and legibly confirm the core claim(s) that plausibly motivated the citation in the first place" — a categorically different, more specific, and more directly actionable framing than generic UX advice.
- **INFERENCE, connecting directly to §2:** because an AI-referred visitor arrives with a *specific* synthesized answer already in mind (e.g., "this company does X for Y audience"), rather than a general browsing intent, their information-scent-driven foraging behavior (§2) is likely anchored to confirming or expanding on that *specific* prior claim — meaning navigation/content that would satisfy a generic first-time visitor's broad orientation needs might still fail this specific visitor if it doesn't legibly connect to the particular claim that brought them there. This is a genuinely different, narrower, more falsifiable hypothesis than "navigation should be clear in general."
- **Evidence gap, explicitly and honestly flagged, consistent with this document series' standing practice (most directly comparable to Topic P's §3 platform-evidence gap):** no controlled study was found in this research pass directly measuring AI-referred-visitor behavior/engagement as distinct from organic-search-referred visitor behavior — this entire unit rests on principled inference from adjacent, independently well-evidenced mechanisms (§1's credibility-speed findings, §2's information-scent theory, and Pulkit's Topic A pipeline-mechanics findings), not on direct empirical measurement of the specific AI-referral case. This should be stated plainly in the skill's own documentation, and is flagged here as a strong candidate for the kind of live-experimentation Pulkit's Topic A A18 (`live-citation-probe`) and Topic R (Experiment Design) are already positioned to investigate — this document's hypothesis is falsifiable and specifically testable by that infrastructure, not merely asserted.

### D. Important mechanisms
The core, hackathon-specific reframing this unit contributes: **the relevant "match" for an AI-referred landing page is not generic clarity, but *specific confirmation of the particular claim that plausibly drove the citation*.** This is a narrower, more diagnostic, more falsifiable target than §1 and §2's general findings alone, and it is the piece of Topic M that most directly justifies treating "on-site engagement" as a distinct hackathon topic rather than simply importing a generic UX audit wholesale.

### E. Concrete website signals
This unit's detection design differs structurally from §1/§2: rather than a purely static, single-page check, it requires knowing (or reasonably inferring) **what specific claim the page is a plausible AI-citation target for**, then checking whether that specific claim is legibly, prominently confirmed above the fold (directly composing with §1's prominence-detection logic) and whether the page's information scent (§2) supports a visitor pursuing that specific claim further, rather than merely being generically well-organized.

### F. How the signal could be detected automatically
Hybrid by necessity, more heavily LLM-dependent than §1/§2 given the semantic-matching task involved: (1) deterministic — extract the page's own apparent core claim/topic (reusing the category/positioning-clarity detection logic Pulkit's Topic A A13 already establishes); (2) where feasible within the runtime and API-cost budget, and explicitly coordinated with (not duplicating) Pulkit's Topic A A18 `live-citation-probe` infrastructure — check whether a live AI-assistant query plausibly matching this page's apparent core claim would cite this page, and if so, what specific framing/claim the citation surfaces; (3) compare that claim's prominence and legibility against §1's above-the-fold detection logic, now scoped specifically to *that* claim rather than a generic value-proposition check. Where live-query infrastructure isn't available within budget, this unit should degrade gracefully to §1/§2's general checks with an explicit note that the AI-referral-specific comparison was not performed, rather than fabricating a confident AI-referral-specific finding from static analysis alone.

### G. What evidence the skill should report
Where live-query data is available: the specific claim a test citation surfaced, and whether/how prominently that specific claim is confirmed on the landing page. Where unavailable: an explicit statement that this unit's AI-referral-specific comparison was not performed within this run's scope, falling back to §1/§2's general findings only.

### H. Possible severity logic
High severity specifically requires the compounded case: a live-query-confirmed citation claim exists, **and** that specific claim is not legibly/prominently confirmed on the landing page (a genuine, specific, AI-referral-driven mismatch) — this is a categorically stronger, more specific finding than §1's general "no value proposition found" finding, and should be labeled/weighted as such. Absent live-query data, this unit should not manufacture severity on its own — it should defer entirely to §1/§2.

### I. Correct remediation
Where a specific mismatch is confirmed: adjust above-the-fold content to legibly and prominently confirm the specific claim(s) driving citations, not merely to improve generic clarity — a materially more targeted, actionable remediation than §1's general guidance.

### J. False-positive cases
A single live-query snapshot (per the same limitation Pulkit's A18 already names explicitly) is not proof of the *only* or even the *typical* claim driving citations for this page — this unit's findings should be framed as "evidence from a limited sample of test queries," directly matching the brief's own explicit warning against treating one experimental run as conclusive ("no citation in one experiment = invisibility" is the brief's named forbidden pattern; the mirror-image overclaim — "this one observed citation framing is definitely representative" — is equally forbidden and must be guarded against here).

### K. False-negative risks
If the live-query infrastructure isn't run (budget/availability constraints), this unit produces no AI-referral-specific finding at all, falling back to general checks — meaning a genuine AI-referral-specific mismatch could exist and go undetected in any run where the live-query component doesn't execute; this should be transparently reported as a scope limitation of that specific run, not silently omitted.

### L. Counterexamples
A page could legitimately be optimized around a *different* primary claim than whatever a single test query happened to surface, without that being any kind of defect — e.g., a product page primarily built around feature X might occasionally be cited for an incidental mention of feature Y; a single mismatch on a secondary claim shouldn't be treated with the same severity as a mismatch on the page's own evidently primary claim (established via §1's own value-proposition-detection logic as a cross-check).

### M. Does this generalize across site types?
The underlying mechanism (referred visitors seek confirmation of the specific claim that drove referral) is general and site-type-agnostic in principle; practical execution depends entirely on live-query infrastructure being available and affordable within the runtime budget, which may itself vary by how the marketplace's final architecture allocates its 5-minute budget across skills — an implementation/Combine-phase question, not resolved here.

### N. Candidate skill(s)
Not a fully standalone skill in its static form — this unit is best implemented as an **enhancement layer on `first-viewport-orientation-check` (§1) that activates when live-query infrastructure (Pulkit's Topic A `live-citation-probe`) is available**, rather than a fully independent skill duplicating crawl/rendering work. This is an explicit architecture recommendation for the Combine phase, not a claim that this must be built as its own skill folder.

### O. Relationship to other potential skills
The most consequential cross-topic dependency in this entire document: this unit is functionally inert without Pulkit's Topic A A18 (`live-citation-probe`) infrastructure, and should be explicitly designed as a downstream consumer of that skill's output rather than reimplementing live-query capability independently — this is also the most direct, concrete bridge between Pulkit's AI-discoverability half of the marketplace and this document's on-site-engagement half, matching the master research map's own explicit framing of "AI-to-Human Handoff" (its Topic X) as "a very good bridge between the two halves of the challenge."

**FINDING ID:** M-01
**Researcher:** Harsh
**Research Area:** M — On-site Engagement
**Research Question:** §1 — is "visitors judge credibility almost instantly" a genuine, peer-reviewed, mechanistically-detailed finding, and does it validate a static, structural audit approach?
**Observation:** Two independent, foundational, peer-reviewed studies establish that visual-appeal judgments form within 50 milliseconds (Lindgaard et al. 2006) and that credibility assessments specifically stabilize within approximately 3.42 seconds (Robins & Holmes 2008) — both far too fast for deliberate content-reading, confirming the judgment is a rapid, holistic, structural/visual gestalt response. A separately peer-reviewed, formal model (Fogg's Prominence-Interpretation Theory) establishes that a credibility signal must be both prominent (noticed) and favorably interpreted to register at all — presence without prominence functions as absence.
**Evidence:** Lindgaard, Fernandes, Dudek & Brown, "Attention web designers: You have 50 milliseconds to make a good first impression!", *Behaviour & Information Technology* (2006); Robins & Holmes (2008), cited within multiple independent peer-reviewed reviews; Fogg et al., Stanford Web Credibility Research, Prominence-Interpretation Theory; a controlled peer-reviewed aesthetics-credibility study demonstrating the "amelioration effect" (identical content, different visual treatment, different credibility judgment).
**Sources:** See §1 §C.
**Pattern:** Because the judgment is fast, holistic, and prominence-gated rather than analytical/content-reading-based, a static, structural, single-snapshot crawl-based audit is a methodologically well-matched approach to this specific phenomenon — a genuine validation of the hackathon's runtime-constrained audit format for this particular check, not merely an assumption of convenience.
**Counterexamples:** The amelioration-effect study also demonstrates this document's structural/content-presence checks cannot measure actual aesthetic execution quality (color, typography, polish) — only presence, positioning, and specificity of content — an honest, explicitly-stated scope limitation.
**Hypothesis:** A structural, above-the-fold, presence-and-prominence-based audit (value proposition specificity, credibility-signal prominence, CTA-commitment-level sequencing) approximates the mechanism these studies measure closely enough to be a valid proxy check, though the specific correlation between our structural score and actual human credibility-judgment outcomes has not itself been directly tested.
**Signal:** Presence, above-the-fold position, and structural prominence of a specific value-proposition statement, basic credibility signals, and the commitment-level of the primary interactive element.
**How to Detect:** Deterministic DOM/viewport-heuristic extraction of above-the-fold content and interactive elements; LLM escalation only for ambiguous value-proposition specificity judgments.
**Evidence Output:** Value-proposition presence/specificity/position; credibility-signal presence-vs-prominence verdict; primary-CTA commitment-level and preceding-context assessment.
**False Positives:** Returning-user/authenticated surfaces and well-known brands relying on recognition rather than explanation should not be flagged under the same standard as a cold, unfamiliar-brand landing page.
**False Negatives:** Above-the-fold detection is viewport-size-dependent; single-viewport-assumption testing could miss mobile-specific failures.
**Severity:** High (no value proposition at all) to Low (minor prominence issues).
**Recommended Fix:** Ensure a specific, concrete value-proposition statement and appropriately-sequenced, commitment-matched CTA are both present and structurally prominent above the fold.
**Generalization:** High — underlying psychological mechanism is universal; specific content expectations are site-type and brand-familiarity conditioned (Topic V hook).
**Candidate Skill:** `first-viewport-orientation-check`
**Related Skills:** Topic F/H/P (same "prominence/legibility, not mere presence, is the real mechanism" pattern applied to a human reader here rather than an AI system), Topic N (shared semantic-HTML/heading-structure detection infrastructure).
**Confidence:** HIGH (the underlying peer-reviewed psychological mechanisms) / MEDIUM (that our specific structural proxy checks are a sufficiently faithful operationalization of what these studies actually measured, which is a genuine, stated, untested assumption).

**FINDING ID:** M-02
**Researcher:** Harsh
**Research Area:** M — On-site Engagement
**Research Question:** §2 — is "information scent"/navigation predictability a formally modeled, peer-reviewed theory with precedented automated-evaluation tooling, or an ad hoc UX intuition?
**Observation:** Information Foraging Theory (Pirolli & Card, *Psychological Review*, 1999) is a genuine, mathematically-formalized cognitive theory directly adapted from optimal foraging theory in behavioral ecology, with a specific, quotable, computable mechanism (trigger-word-driven spreading activation predicting link-following behavior) and multiple decades of peer-reviewed refinement. The theory has directly produced working automated usability-evaluation tools (BLOODHOUND, the Cognitive Walkthrough for the Web), confirming automated, structural evaluation of information scent is a precedented, solved category of tool, not a speculative undertaking.
**Evidence:** Pirolli & Card, "Information Foraging," *Psychological Review* 106(4) (1999); Card, Pirolli, Van Der Wege et al., CHI 2001; Chi, Rosien, Suppattanasiri et al. (BLOODHOUND), CHI 2003; Blackmon, Polson, Kitajima, Lewis (Cognitive Walkthrough for the Web).
**Sources:** See §2 §C.
**Pattern:** The theory's own operational unit (semantic/trigger-word overlap between anchor text and destination content) is directly, deterministically computable, and — independently and elegantly — converges with Pulkit's own Topic A A16 finding (heading-to-content topical alignment as a machine chunk-retrieval quality signal): the same underlying "does the label predict the content" mechanism, evidenced independently for both human wayfinding and machine retrieval.
**Counterexamples:** Genuine synonymy (a label and its destination are semantically aligned but lexically non-overlapping) is a false-positive risk for a purely deterministic keyword-based first pass; deliberately evocative/brand-voice navigation labeling is a legitimate design choice in specific genre contexts, not a universal defect.
**Hypothesis:** Semantic-overlap scoring between primary navigation anchor text and destination-page title/heading content is a valid, theory-grounded proxy for information-scent strength, with severity weighted toward high-traffic primary navigation over incidental in-content links (which the theory itself treats as lower-stakes and more associative).
**Signal:** Semantic alignment score between nav/link anchor text and destination-page title/H1/summary content.
**How to Detect:** Deterministic keyword-overlap first pass (template-level, not per-page, sharing crawl infrastructure with Pulkit's A22/Soham's AF template-clustering), LLM escalation for borderline/ambiguous pairs.
**Evidence Output:** Flagged low-scent nav items with anchor text, destination, alignment score, and expected-vs-actual content example.
**False Positives:** Playful/brand-voice labels with adequate non-textual scent (icons, position, brief descriptions) should not be flagged; genre-appropriate evocative labeling (editorial/creative sites) should be scored conservatively.
**False Negatives:** Deterministic keyword matching misses genuine synonymy; runtime-budget-limited LLM escalation may not catch every case.
**Severity:** Medium-High for primary/main navigation; Low for incidental in-content links.
**Recommended Fix:** Rewrite navigation labels using specific trigger words genuinely matching destination content.
**Generalization:** High for the mechanism; specific label-literalness expectations are genre/site-type conditioned (Topic V hook).
**Candidate Skill:** `navigation-scent-check`
**Related Skills:** Pulkit's Topic A (A16, A22) — strong candidate for explicit co-design given the mechanism convergence.
**Confidence:** HIGH — both the underlying theory's peer-reviewed standing and its direct precedent in automated-evaluation tooling are well-established, not inferred.
