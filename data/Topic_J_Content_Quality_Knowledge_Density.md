## §1 — Specificity and vagueness as a formally measurable linguistic property

### A. What we need to understand
Is "generic marketing language" versus "specific, evidenced content" a real, formally-defined, linguistically-grounded construct with an established measurement methodology — or is "avoid vague marketing fluff" simply reasonable-sounding editorial advice being dressed up as a technical check? Can vagueness genuinely be scored deterministically, at the lexical level, rather than requiring a subjective LLM judgment for every sentence?

### B. Why it matters for the hackathon
If a real, peer-reviewed, deterministic vagueness-scoring methodology exists, it directly satisfies one of the brief's own explicit priority items ("false-positive reduction") in an unusually strong way: a lexicon-based, symbolic scoring approach is fully deterministic, fully explainable (every flagged word is individually inspectable), and requires no LLM call at all for the base scoring pass — a genuinely rare case in this document series where the highest-rigor approach is also the cheapest and most runtime-friendly one, rather than requiring a tradeoff between the two.

### C. Current evidence
- **FACT (peer-reviewed, foundational, directly on point, the single most load-bearing citation in this document):** VAGO is a real, published, symbolic NLP system (Bertaux, Anantharaman & colleagues; documented across arXiv 2110.14780 (originally in a fake-news detection context), arXiv 2309.06132 ("Measuring vagueness and subjectivity in texts: from symbolic to neural VAGO"), and independently used/evaluated in a 2024 CLEF CheckThat! shared-task paper, arXiv 2407.03770) that computes three distinct, formally-defined scores for a given sentence: **vagueness** (the ratio of vague words to total words in the sentence, using a curated lexical database of 1,640 terms across English and French, categorized into distinct vagueness sub-types), **subjectivity** (proportion of subjective versus objective vocabulary), and **detail** (based on named-entity density — the paper's own stated assumption is that named entities "ground the information reported in specific objects and generally leave very limited room for variable interpretation. The more named entities, the more detailed the sentence is likely to be"). This is a directly quotable, formally-defined, peer-reviewed methodology — not an invented heuristic — with a working reference implementation (built on the GATE NLP framework and spaCy for named-entity recognition) and a public demo tool, meaning this is a genuinely precedented, reproducible approach this hackathon could adapt rather than design from scratch.
- **FACT (peer-reviewed, directly confirming the detail/specificity half of this construct independently):** the same CheckThat! 2024 paper states the detail-and-objectivity measurement mechanism explicitly: named entities (people, locations, temporal indications, institutions, numbers) are extracted via spaCy, and sentence "detail" is scored based on named-entity presence/density — this is a directly reusable, deterministic technique (off-the-shelf NER, no custom model training required) that operationalizes exactly the "specific claim vs. generic claim" distinction the brief's own materials use as a worked example (comparing "we help businesses grow" against "our platform automates payroll for companies with 10–500 employees" — the second sentence scores meaningfully higher on named-entity/numeric density than the first, a direct, testable prediction of this methodology).
- **FACT (peer-reviewed, on the closely related but analytically distinct construct of hedging):** hedging — "words and phrases that display a sense of vagueness or that lack precision, such as suppose, about" — is independently studied as a distinct linguistic marker (cited in deception-detection research, Springer 2021) with its own automated detection methods (rule-based systems trained on syntactic features). This is worth noting as a related-but-formally-distinct construct from VAGO's "vagueness" category: hedging concerns *epistemic commitment* (how confidently a claim is asserted) while VAGO's core vagueness lexicon concerns *semantic precision* (how specific the referents/quantities are) — both are real, independently studied phenomena, and a comprehensive specificity check should account for both rather than assuming they're the same thing.
- **FACT (peer-reviewed, a genuinely important 2026 methodological finding directly relevant to detection-approach choice):** a 2026 study on "managerial obfuscation" in corporate disclosures explicitly critiques simple bag-of-words/fixed-dictionary approaches to vagueness/tonal analysis, noting they "merely count key words without interpreting context, intent, or rhetorical strategy" and therefore "often conflate technical complexity inherent in the business with obfuscation and fail to distinguish syntactic difficulty from semantic obfuscation." This paper's own solution is an LLM-based measurement approach validated for "high reliability across independent scoring runs." This is directly, honestly relevant to this document's design choice: **a pure lexicon-based approach (VAGO's core method) risks a real, documented false-positive mode** — flagging genuinely necessary technical/domain-specific complexity as "vague" simply because it doesn't match a general-purpose vagueness lexicon, or conversely missing genuine obfuscation that uses technically precise-sounding but substantively empty language. This is a legitimate, evidence-backed argument for this document's design to use VAGO's lexical approach as a fast, deterministic **first-pass filter**, with LLM escalation reserved for borderline or high-stakes cases — directly consistent with, and now further strengthened by an additional independent source for, this document series' standing hybrid-approach discipline.
- **FACT (Google, first-party, direct, on the actual real-world consequence of "generic/thin" content — establishing this isn't merely an academic curiosity):** Matt Cutts (then head of Google's webspam/search-quality team), quoted directly and consistently across independent secondary sources: **"Worthless content doesn't hurt your website, it hurts your rankings... it will not rank in the search engine. It may not even be indexed."** While this is a secondary-sourced quote (not independently verified against a primary Google transcript in this research pass), the underlying claim it makes — that genuinely low-substance content faces an indexing/ranking penalty distinct from any technical defect — is consistent with Google's own current, directly-documented "Creating Helpful, Reliable, People-First Content" guidance (already cited in the master research map's own Topic J section), and is treated as OBSERVATION-tier (widely and consistently attributed, directionally consistent with current official guidance) rather than FACT-tier given the lack of a directly-verified primary transcript in this specific research pass.

### D. Important mechanisms
Two compounding, evidenced mechanisms: **(1) specificity/detail and vagueness are formally, independently measurable via named-entity density and a curated vagueness lexicon respectively** — a real, published, symbolic methodology, not an invented heuristic; and **(2) pure lexicon-based scoring has a documented false-positive risk of conflating necessary domain complexity with genuine vagueness**, meaning the correct design (per §C's 2026 obfuscation-measurement finding) is a fast deterministic first pass with judgment-based escalation for ambiguous cases — directly reinforcing, via an entirely independent research lineage, the same hybrid-approach discipline this document series has applied in every prior topic.

### E. Concrete website signals
For claim-bearing sentences on key pages (product/pricing/about/positioning pages, reusing the page-scoping logic already established in Pulkit's A17 for "pages whose evident purpose is factual/comparative"): named-entity and numeric-value density (a direct, deterministic detail/specificity proxy); ratio of general-purpose vague-lexicon terms (adaptable from VAGO's published category structure, or a comparably-constructed English lexicon) to total words; presence of hedging markers on claims that would normally be expected to be concrete (e.g., a pricing statement using "approximately," "typically," "in most cases" where a specific figure would be expected and is not obviously justified by genuine variability).

### F. How the signal could be detected automatically
Deterministic-first, directly reusable and cheap: (1) run off-the-shelf named-entity recognition (spaCy or equivalent, directly following VAGO's own published implementation approach) over claim-bearing page sections and compute an entity/numeric-density score per sentence or per chunk (reusing the chunk-window infrastructure already established in Pulkit's A16); (2) maintain a compact, curated vagueness-lexicon word list (adapted from VAGO's published methodology, or a comparable general-purpose list) and compute a vague-word ratio per sentence, directly following VAGO's own formally-defined scoring formula; (3) flag sentences scoring low on entity/numeric density AND high on vague-word ratio as candidate generic/vague content; (4) escalate only borderline or high-stakes-page candidates (per §C's obfuscation-measurement finding) to a single LLM judgment specifically checking whether apparent vagueness reflects genuine necessary complexity (e.g., a legitimately nuanced legal/medical caveat) versus actual evasiveness or emptiness.

### G. What evidence the skill should report
For flagged sentences/sections: the specific vague terms and their category (per VAGO's own category taxonomy, adaptable for English), the named-entity/numeric-density score, and — for the specific worked-example pattern the brief itself uses — a direct before/after contrast showing what a more specific version of the same claim would look like (reusing Pulkit's own A16/A17 "self-contained fact statement" framing as the target state).

### H. Possible severity logic
- **Medium-High:** a page whose evident purpose is factual/comparative/decision-relevant (pricing, product-spec, comparison pages — reusing Pulkit's A17 page-scoping) is dominated by high-vagueness, low-specificity language on its core claims — directly undermining the page's ability to supply the kind of explicit, unhedged, quotable statements Pulkit's A17 findings show are associated with citation competitiveness.
- **Low:** vagueness detected on pages/sections where some generality is legitimately expected (a mission statement, a values page, marketing copy not making specific factual claims) — this must not be flagged at the same severity, per the false-positive guardrail below.
- **Informational only, framed as a proactive suggestion:** minor, isolated instances of hedging/vague language that don't dominate a page's core claims.

### I. Correct remediation
Replace vague, generic claims on factual/comparative pages with specific, quantified, named-entity-bearing statements (directly following the brief's own "we help businesses grow" → "our platform automates payroll for companies with 10–500 employees" worked contrast) — a remediation directly reusable from and consistent with Pulkit's own A16/A17 findings, since a more specific claim is, by construction, also typically a more chunk-safe, self-contained, quotable one.

### J. False-positive cases
Genuinely necessary domain complexity, appropriate hedging on claims that are legitimately uncertain or conditional (medical, legal, financial content where precise universal claims would themselves be inaccurate or irresponsible), and non-factual content types (mission statements, brand narrative, opinion/editorial content) must not be flagged under the same standard as factual/comparative claims — this is the central, explicitly-evidenced risk identified in §C's obfuscation-measurement source, and the detection design's page-scoping and LLM-escalation steps exist specifically to guard against it. A page using precise technical/industry jargon for an expert audience (directly reusing the false-positive guardrail already established in Pulkit's own A12 finding on jargon vs. plain-language synonyms) should not be flagged as "vague" merely because a general-purpose vagueness lexicon doesn't recognize its specialized vocabulary as specific.

### K. False-negative risks
A curated vagueness lexicon, built primarily from published general-purpose sources (per VAGO's own English/French lexicon), may not capture domain-specific or newly-emerging vague marketing terminology not yet reflected in the reference lexicon — this requires periodic maintenance, similar in kind to the schema-property reference-table maintenance risk already flagged in Topic G §2.

### L. Counterexamples
A short, simple factual statement using no named entities or numbers at all can still be perfectly specific and non-vague in context (e.g., "Returns are accepted within 30 days" contains a specific number and would score well; "We're here to help" contains neither and would correctly score as vague) — but a claim like "Our support team responds quickly" is genuinely vague by this methodology's own standard even though it might read as reasonably informative to a casual human reader, illustrating that this methodology's threshold for "vague" is stricter and more literal than everyday human editorial judgment, a limitation worth stating honestly rather than implying the score perfectly tracks subjective content-quality perception.

### M. Does this generalize across site types?
Yes, for the underlying measurement methodology (named-entity density, vagueness-lexicon ratio are general linguistic techniques, language-dependent but not site-type-dependent). The severity/relevance of a vagueness finding is genuinely site-type and page-type dependent (per the false-positive guardrail above) — a stronger and more central site-type dependency than in most prior topics in this series, since some entire site genres (creative/editorial, brand-narrative-heavy sites) legitimately and appropriately use less specific, more evocative language throughout, not just on isolated pages.

### N. Candidate skill(s)
**`content-substance-check`** — the primary detection skill for this unit, built around a VAGO-inspired deterministic lexical scoring pipeline with LLM escalation reserved for borderline/high-stakes cases.

### O. Relationship to other potential skills
Directly complements, without duplicating, Pulkit's A16/A17 (which assess whether a fact is *structurally* self-contained/chunk-safe) — this unit assesses whether the fact, wherever it sits structurally, is *substantively* specific and evidenced in the first place; a page could pass A16's structural check (a claim is self-contained within a chunk-safe window) while still failing this unit's substance check (the self-contained claim itself is generic and empty), and these should be reported as two distinct findings rather than one skill silently absorbing both concerns. Also directly reuses page-scoping logic already established in Pulkit's A12/A17 (jargon false-positive guard; factual/comparative page scoping).

## §2 — Duplication and thinness as a distinct, separately-evidenced mechanism

### A. What we need to understand
Is "duplicate/near-duplicate content" a solved, well-precedented technical detection problem with established, cheap, deterministic algorithms — and does the evidence genuinely support treating *length* and *genuine informational uniqueness* as two entirely separate axes, directly enforcing the brief's own explicit warning against both "long = good" and "short = bad" assumptions?

### B. Why it matters
If near-duplicate detection is a mature, well-precedented technical field (not something this hackathon would need to invent), this is a low-implementation-risk, high-confidence check to include. And if the evidence clearly separates "length" from "uniqueness" as independent axes, this document can give the marketplace a concrete, technically-grounded reason to reject the brief's named forbidden assumptions rather than merely asserting the rule by fiat.

### C. Current evidence
- **FACT (well-established computer science, foundational, directly on point):** near-duplicate text detection via **MinHash** and **SimHash** — locality-sensitive hashing techniques that estimate document similarity using compact hash signatures rather than full pairwise text comparison — is a mature, decades-old, widely-deployed technique, explicitly confirmed as used by major search engines for exactly this purpose. **W-shingling** (comparing overlapping n-gram "shingles," typically 5-word windows, via Jaccard similarity) is the standard underlying representation these hashing techniques operate on. This is genuinely solved, low-risk, deterministic, well-documented technology — not a novel or speculative technique this hackathon would need to develop.
- **FACT (peer-reviewed, directly on point, a rigorous recent benchmark comparison):** RETSim ("Resilient and Efficient Text Similarity," arXiv 2311.17264) is a peer-reviewed paper directly benchmarking MinHash, SimHash, and newer embedding-based methods against real-world near-duplicate datasets (NEWS-COPY: 27,210 historical news articles with 122,876 labeled duplicate pairs; CORE: 100k scholarly articles with labeled exact/near-duplicate/non-duplicate pairs) — confirming this is an actively-benchmarked field with real ground-truth datasets, not merely a plausible-sounding technique. The paper's own finding that MinHash's relative performance depends on the length ratio between compared documents is a genuinely useful, specific, primary-source-grounded implementation detail (not something a generic "use MinHash" recommendation would surface) directly relevant to calibrating this hackathon's own detection thresholds.
- **FACT (peer-reviewed, directly relevant methodological finding — a specific, non-obvious implementation risk worth flagging on its own):** Fröbe, Hagen et al. ("The Impact of Main Content Extraction on Near-Duplicate Detection") is a peer-reviewed paper directly studying exactly the implementation risk this hackathon's own crawling infrastructure would face: comparing near-duplicate detection accuracy using full page content versus extracted main-content-only, finding that **"full content of pages serves precision-oriented near-duplicate-detection, while main content extraction is more recall-oriented."** This is a directly useful, primary-source-grounded design tradeoff: if this hackathon's crawler strips boilerplate (nav/footer/ads) before duplicate-checking — which several other skills in this document series already assume as standard practice (Pulkit's A22 template-clustering, this series' own repeated "main content extraction" references) — the resulting duplicate-detection pass will be more recall-oriented (catches more true duplicates, including partial/templated similarity) but potentially less precision-oriented (more prone to over-flagging pages that share only boilerplate-adjacent structure) — a genuinely non-obvious, evidence-grounded tradeoff worth stating explicitly in this skill's design documentation rather than assuming either approach is simply "correct."
- **FACT, directly confirming the brief's own forbidden-assumption warning from the technical literature itself, independent of any SEO-content framing:** the industry-standard operational definition (independently, consistently stated across multiple sources describing real production near-duplicate-detection systems) uses a **similarity-percentage threshold**, not a length threshold — "Pages with 90%+ similarity are flagged as duplicates, and 60-90% as near-duplicates... below 60% is considered unique" — meaning the entire technical apparatus this document proposes measures **overlap between documents**, a completely different axis from either document's absolute length. A very short page and a very long page are each independently and identically checkable for duplication against other pages using this method — length simply never enters the calculation, which is a clean, structural, technique-level confirmation (not merely a policy assertion) that length and uniqueness are unrelated axes.
- **OBSERVATION, directly and usefully on point for what "thin content" actually means when correctly specified (not simply "short"):** Google's own current, directly-documented "Creating Helpful, Reliable, People-First Content" guidance (already correctly cited in the master research map's own Topic J framing) explicitly distinguishes thin content from short content — the concrete illustrative case repeatedly given across independent sources is a genuinely long page (e.g., 2,000 words) that is nonetheless "thin" because it **repeats the same information** rather than being substantively repetitive-free — directly confirming that "thinness," correctly defined, is a **redundancy/uniqueness-of-information-per-word** measure, not a raw word-count threshold, and that a long page can fail this exact test as easily as (or more easily than) a short one, a direct, concrete illustration of why "long = good" is specifically and structurally wrong, not merely generally advised against.
- **FACT (peer-reviewed, a genuinely important nuance for detecting *templated/programmatic* thin content specifically, a distinct sub-case named explicitly in the master research map, J44):** Weissman, Ayhan, Bradley & Lin ("Identifying Duplicate and Contradictory Information in Wikipedia") apply MinHash-based near-duplicate sentence detection within a single large corpus and find that near-duplicate clusters can be meaningfully categorized, with two categories flagged as "particularly interesting": exact copy-paste duplication, and — directly relevant to this document series' own Topic P/H work on conflicting information — **near-duplicate sentences that state contradictory facts**, which the paper explicitly notes "point to quality issues." This is a valuable, independently-arrived-at confirmation that near-duplicate detection technique, applied *within* a single site's own pages (rather than only against external sources), can surface not just redundancy but genuine internal *contradiction* between near-identical passages — a distinct, higher-severity finding worth designing for specifically, and one that directly connects this unit's technique back to Topic P's and Topic H's own contradiction-detection concerns, but applied intra-site rather than cross-source.

### D. Important mechanisms
Two compounding, evidenced points: **(1) duplicate/near-duplicate detection is a mature, precedented, deterministic technique measuring content overlap, structurally independent of document length** — meaning this hackathon does not need to invent new methodology, only correctly apply an established one; and **(2) "thin content," correctly defined by Google's own current guidance, is specifically a redundancy/uniqueness-of-information measure, not a length threshold** — directly and structurally falsifying the "long = good, short = bad" assumption pair the brief explicitly names, using the same technical measurement approach (overlap/similarity scoring) for both the cross-page duplication case and the within-page repetition case.

### E. Concrete website signals
Pairwise near-duplicate similarity scores (via w-shingling + MinHash/SimHash, per §C) across a site's own pages (intra-site duplication/cannibalization, and templated/programmatic thin-content detection per J44), and, where feasible within the runtime budget, against a small number of external pages (cross-site plagiarism/syndication detection — directly connecting to and reusable by Topic H §1's syndication-ratio detection, which already flagged needing exactly this kind of near-duplicate-text check for its own corroboration-independence scoring); within-page internal repetition (does a single long page restate the same specific fact/claim multiple times using near-identical phrasing, using the same shingling technique applied intra-document rather than across documents).

### F. How the signal could be detected automatically
Deterministic, fully precedented: extract main content (per the crawl infrastructure already established across this series, with the explicit precision/recall tradeoff from Fröbe et al. noted in the skill's own documentation); compute w-shingling (5-word n-grams, per the established convention) and MinHash/SimHash signatures per page; compute pairwise Jaccard similarity across the site's own crawled pages, flagging 90%+ as duplicate and 60–90% as near-duplicate (directly reusing the established industry-convention thresholds cited in §C, with an explicit note that these are convention-derived starting points, not independently re-validated by this research pass, and should be treated as adjustable parameters); separately, run the same technique within individual long pages to detect internal repetition (the "2,000-word page repeating itself" case). No LLM escalation is required for the core similarity computation itself (fully deterministic per the established technique); LLM escalation is reserved only for characterizing *why* two near-duplicate passages are near-duplicate when that's non-obvious (e.g., distinguishing legitimate templated-but-differentiated content, per the false-positive guardrail below, from genuine low-effort duplication).

### G. What evidence the skill should report
For each detected duplicate/near-duplicate pair or cluster: the specific pages/sections involved, the similarity score, and a classification (exact/near-duplicate, cross-page or intra-page, and — reusing Weissman et al.'s categorization — whether the near-duplicate passages are genuinely redundant or, more seriously, near-identical in structure but **contradictory in content** (a distinct, higher-severity sub-case).

### H. Possible severity logic
- **High:** near-duplicate passages that are structurally similar but factually contradictory (per Weissman et al.'s categorization) — a genuine, distinct quality/trust issue, not merely a redundancy inefficiency, and one that connects directly to this document series' own Topic H/P contradiction-detection work when the contradiction spans a claim type those topics' materiality taxonomy would flag as decision-relevant.
- **Medium:** substantial cross-page near-duplication suggesting programmatic/templated thin content (J44) across many pages of a site — a template-level, site-wide finding per this series' standing infrastructure (Pulkit's A22 template-clustering), not a per-page one.
- **Low:** minor within-page repetition on a single page, or boilerplate-adjacent similarity between pages that share legitimate structural templates without genuine content-level redundancy (see false positives below).

### I. Correct remediation
Consolidate or meaningfully differentiate near-duplicate pages (directly following Google's own current guidance's framing); for internal repetition, tighten a page's own content to state each fact once, clearly, rather than restating it multiple times without adding new information; for contradictory near-duplicates specifically, resolve which version is correct and remove or correct the other, directly reusing this document series' own directionality-reporting discipline from Topic P.

### J. False-positive cases
Shared boilerplate (navigation, footer, legitimate templated structural elements) must be excluded before similarity scoring, or every page on a site will trivially register as "near-duplicate" of every other page purely from shared chrome — this is precisely why main-content extraction (with the explicit precision/recall tradeoff noted in §C) is a necessary precondition for this check, not an optional refinement. Legitimately similar but intentionally-templated content that is nonetheless genuinely differentiated in its specific substance (e.g., a large e-commerce catalog's product pages sharing a template and boilerplate description structure, but each describing a genuinely distinct product with distinct specifications) should not be flagged as problematic near-duplication merely for sharing structure — the check must specifically measure content-level, not template-level, similarity, and should escalate borderline cases to LLM judgment specifically to distinguish "same template, different substance" from "same template, same/near-same substance."

### K. False-negative risks
Sophisticated paraphrasing/rewriting (including AI-generated "spun" variants of the same underlying content) can evade lexical-overlap-based detection (w-shingling/MinHash) while still being substantively redundant — the source material itself notes that "spinning text — manually or with AI — to create superficially different versions doesn't fool modern search engines," implying more sophisticated (likely embedding-based/semantic) detection exists in production search systems beyond what a simple lexical-overlap check would catch; this document's proposed deterministic-first approach is a reasonable, cheap, runtime-appropriate baseline but should be explicitly documented as having this specific, named limitation, rather than presented as a complete duplicate-content solution.

### L. Counterexamples
A site with many genuinely short, genuinely unique, non-redundant pages (e.g., a well-organized FAQ or glossary, where each entry is necessarily brief but covers entirely distinct ground) should score well on this unit's tests despite low individual page length — directly and concretely validating, rather than merely asserting, the brief's "short ≠ bad" principle using this unit's own actual measurement methodology (low cross-page similarity, high per-page named-entity/specificity from §1) rather than treating it as an unenforced disclaimer.

### M. Does this generalize across site types?
Yes, fully and cleanly — the underlying detection technique is content-agnostic and site-type-agnostic; the only site-type dependency is in interpreting *why* duplication exists (a large e-commerce catalog will legitimately have more structural similarity across pages than a blog, and severity calibration should account for this, a now-familiar Topic V hook consistent with every prior topic).

### N. Candidate skill(s)

### O. Relationship to other potential skills

**FINDING ID:** J-01
**Researcher:** Harsh
**Research Area:** J — Content Quality & Knowledge Density
**Research Question:** §1 — is "generic vs. specific" content a formally, deterministically measurable linguistic construct, and what design does the evidence support?
**Observation:** VAGO is a real, peer-reviewed, symbolic NLP system computing formally-defined vagueness (curated lexicon ratio), subjectivity, and detail (named-entity density) scores, with a working public implementation. A separate, independent 2026 study on measuring corporate-disclosure obfuscation explicitly documents a real false-positive risk in pure lexicon-based vagueness scoring (conflating necessary domain complexity with genuine vagueness) and validates an LLM-assisted approach for higher reliability — directly supporting a deterministic-first-pass-plus-LLM-escalation hybrid design.
**Evidence:** VAGO papers (arXiv 2110.14780, arXiv 2309.06132, arXiv 2407.03770/CheckThat! 2024); "Beyond Fog: Measuring managerial obfuscation using LLMs," ScienceDirect (2026); hedging-detection literature (Springer, 2021) as a related-but-distinct construct.
**Sources:** See §1 §C.
**Pattern:** Specificity/vagueness is not merely an editorial preference — it is an established, peer-reviewed, symbolically-scoreable linguistic property (named-entity/numeric density for specificity, curated-lexicon ratio for vagueness), directly operationalizing the brief's own "we help businesses grow" vs. "our platform automates payroll for companies with 10-500 employees" worked contrast into a concrete, testable, deterministic metric.
**Counterexamples:** Necessary domain complexity, legitimate hedging on genuinely uncertain/conditional claims (medical/legal/financial), and non-factual content types (mission statements, brand narrative) must not be flagged under this methodology's literal standard, which is stricter than everyday human editorial judgment.
**Hypothesis:** A VAGO-inspired deterministic first-pass (named-entity/numeric density + curated vagueness-lexicon ratio), scoped to factual/comparative pages (reusing Pulkit's A17 page-scoping), with LLM escalation reserved for borderline/high-stakes cases, will surface genuine generic-language findings at meaningfully lower false-positive risk than either a pure-lexicon or pure-LLM approach alone.
**Signal:** Named-entity/numeric density per claim-bearing sentence/chunk; vague-lexicon-term ratio per sentence; hedging-marker presence on claims where specificity would normally be expected.
**How to Detect:** Deterministic off-the-shelf NER (spaCy or equivalent) + curated vagueness lexicon scoring (VAGO-methodology-derived); LLM escalation only for borderline/high-stakes-page candidates.
**Evidence Output:** Flagged vague terms/category, entity-density score, before/after specificity contrast example.
**False Positives:** Domain-appropriate jargon, legitimate hedging on genuinely uncertain claims, and non-factual content types must not be flagged at the same standard as factual/comparative claims.
**False Negatives:** A static, general-purpose vagueness lexicon requires maintenance and may miss domain-specific or newly-emerging vague terminology.
**Severity:** Medium-High (vagueness dominating a factual/comparative page's core claims) to Informational (isolated instances, or legitimately general content types).
**Recommended Fix:** Replace vague claims with specific, quantified, named-entity-bearing statements on factual/comparative pages.
**Generalization:** High for the measurement methodology (language-dependent, not site-type-dependent); severity/relevance calibration is more strongly site-type/page-type-dependent than most prior topics in this series, since some site genres legitimately use less specific language throughout.
**Candidate Skill:** `content-substance-check`
**Related Skills:** Pulkit's Topic A (A12 jargon false-positive guard, A16/A17 chunk-safety and quotability — complementary, not duplicative: this checks substance, A16/A17 checks structural positioning of that substance).
**Confidence:** HIGH (the underlying peer-reviewed methodology and its documented false-positive risk) / MEDIUM (that this document's proposed English-language adaptation of a lexicon originally built/validated primarily in a French/English bilingual fake-news-detection context transfers cleanly to the general web-content-quality domain, an explicitly stated, untested adaptation assumption).

**FINDING ID:** J-02
**Researcher:** Harsh
**Research Area:** J — Content Quality & Knowledge Density
**Research Question:** §2 — is near-duplicate/thin-content detection a mature, precedented technique, and does the evidence structurally separate "length" from "genuine uniqueness" as the brief requires?
**Observation:** MinHash/SimHash/w-shingling are mature, decades-old, benchmark-evaluated techniques for near-duplicate text detection, confirmed via real labeled datasets (NEWS-COPY, CORE) in a 2023 peer-reviewed benchmark paper (RETSim). A separate peer-reviewed paper directly studies the precision/recall tradeoff between full-page and main-content-only extraction for this exact detection task. Google's own current content-quality guidance's illustrative example of "thin content" is explicitly a long (2,000-word) page that repeats itself — a direct, concrete confirmation that thinness is a redundancy measure, structurally independent of length, not a word-count threshold.
**Evidence:** RETSim, arXiv 2311.17264; Fröbe, Hagen et al., "The Impact of Main Content Extraction on Near-Duplicate Detection," arXiv 2111.10864; Weissman, Ayhan, Bradley & Lin, "Identifying Duplicate and Contradictory Information in Wikipedia," arXiv 1406.1143; Google's current people-first-content guidance (cross-referenced from the master research map's own citation).
**Sources:** See §2 §C.
**Pattern:** The technical measurement apparatus for duplication (pairwise content-overlap similarity via shingling/hashing) structurally never references document length at all — this is not merely a policy assertion that length shouldn't matter, but a technique-level fact that length literally does not enter the calculation, providing this document a structural, evidence-based refutation of the brief's named "long=good/short=bad" forbidden assumptions rather than a mere restatement of the rule.
**Counterexamples:** Legitimately templated-but-differentiated content (e.g., e-commerce product pages sharing structure but describing genuinely distinct products) must not be flagged as problematic duplication merely for sharing template structure; main-content extraction (required to avoid trivial boilerplate-driven false positives) introduces its own documented precision/recall tradeoff that must be stated explicitly, not assumed away.
**Hypothesis:** A w-shingling + MinHash/SimHash pairwise similarity pass, applied both across a site's own pages (cross-page duplication/cannibalization) and within individual long pages (internal repetition), using industry-convention similarity thresholds (90%+ duplicate, 60-90% near-duplicate) as adjustable starting parameters, will correctly and cheaply identify genuine thin/duplicate content without needing length as an input variable at all.
**Signal:** Pairwise content-overlap similarity score (cross-page and intra-page); classification of near-duplicate clusters as redundant versus structurally-similar-but-contradictory (per Weissman et al.'s categorization).
**How to Detect:** Deterministic main-content extraction + w-shingling + MinHash/SimHash signature comparison; LLM escalation only to distinguish legitimate templated differentiation from genuine low-effort duplication in borderline cases.
**Evidence Output:** Specific duplicate/near-duplicate pages or passages, similarity score, redundant-vs-contradictory classification.
**False Positives:** Shared boilerplate must be excluded via main-content extraction before scoring; legitimately templated-but-differentiated content (large catalogs) must not be flagged for sharing structure alone.
**False Negatives:** Sophisticated paraphrasing/AI-spun content-variant evasion of lexical-overlap detection is a named, real limitation of this deterministic-first approach, requiring more sophisticated (likely embedding-based) methods this document does not propose building, given runtime constraints.
**Severity:** High (structurally-similar-but-contradictory near-duplicates) to Medium (programmatic/templated thin content, site-wide) to Low (isolated internal repetition or boilerplate-adjacent similarity).
**Recommended Fix:** Consolidate/differentiate near-duplicate pages; tighten internally-repetitive pages to state facts once; resolve and correct contradictory near-duplicate passages.
**Generalization:** High for the technique itself; interpretation of *why* duplication exists is site-type-dependent (large catalogs vs. blogs), a now-consistent Topic V hook across this entire document series.
**Candidate Skill:** Folds into `content-substance-check` alongside Finding J-01, as a second, technically distinct sub-check sharing the same crawl/extraction infrastructure.
**Related Skills:** Topic H §1 (directly reusable syndication-ratio detection technique, applied cross-domain rather than intra-domain); Topic P (contradictory-near-duplicate sub-case connects directly to Topic P's and Topic H's own cross-source contradiction-detection work, now extended intra-site); Pulkit's A22 (template-clustering infrastructure, directly reusable for scoping programmatic thin-content detection to site-wide findings rather than per-page).
**Confidence:** HIGH — based on a mature, decades-old, actively peer-reviewed-and-benchmarked technical field with real labeled datasets, among the most technically de-risked findings in this entire document series.

## 3. Required end-of-topic synthesis

**Strongest validated insight:**
Content specificity/vagueness and content duplication/thinness are each independently, formally, and deterministically measurable using real, peer-reviewed, precedented techniques (VAGO's lexical/entity-density scoring for the former; MinHash/SimHash/w-shingling for the latter) — and, critically, **neither technique requires document length as an input variable at all.** This is a structural, technique-level fact, not merely a policy assertion, that directly and concretely refutes the brief's own named forbidden assumptions ("long content = good," "short content = bad") using the actual measurement methodology itself, rather than requiring this document to simply assert the rule and hope the detection design happens to comply with it.

**Strongest unvalidated hypothesis:**
That a VAGO-methodology-derived vagueness lexicon, originally developed and validated primarily in a fake-news/misinformation-detection research context (and bilingually, for English and French), will transfer cleanly and without significant adaptation to the general web-content-quality/marketing-language domain this hackathon actually needs. The underlying *technique* (lexicon-ratio + named-entity-density scoring) is well-evidenced; the specific *lexicon content* appropriate for "marketing vagueness" versus "misinformation-adjacent vagueness" has not been independently validated by this research pass and should be treated as an adaptation requiring its own lightweight validation, not an out-of-the-box drop-in.

**Strongest candidate skill:**
`content-substance-check`, combining Finding J-01's specificity/vagueness scoring and Finding J-02's duplication/thinness detection as two technically distinct but naturally co-located sub-checks — both sharing the same main-content-extraction crawl infrastructure this document series has established repeatedly, and both directly complementing (never duplicating) Pulkit's own A16/A17 structural chunk-safety work, per the explicit boundary established at the top of this document.

**Weakest assumption we should investigate next:**
The industry-convention similarity thresholds this document proposes adopting (90%+ duplicate, 60-90% near-duplicate) are drawn from general-purpose commercial tooling descriptions, not independently re-derived or validated against this hackathon's own specific use case (brand-website auditing, as opposed to the plagiarism-detection or search-engine-deduplication contexts these thresholds were originally established for) — before finalizing `content-substance-check`'s detection thresholds, these should be sanity-tested against a small number of real sites with known, deliberately-varied levels of genuine content duplication, consistent with this document series' now-repeated practice (Topic F's collision-detector, Topic P's materiality taxonomy, Topic T's classification calibration) of flagging convention-derived thresholds as provisional pending lightweight empirical testing.
