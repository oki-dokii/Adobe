## 1. FACT vs OBSERVATION vs HYPOTHESIS vs INFERENCE vs SPECULATION — global legend

Same legend as every prior topic in this series:
- **FACT** — documented by the vendor/standards body, or established in peer-reviewed/archival literature.
- **OBSERVATION** — reported by independent researchers/practitioners running real, methodologically transparent trials (not vendor-confirmed).
- **HYPOTHESIS** — our proposed testable claim, not yet validated by us.
- **INFERENCE** — reasonable derivation from FACT/OBSERVATION, one logical step removed.
- **SPECULATION** — plausible but unconfirmed; flagged, not built on.

**A note on source quality, continuing this series' standing practice — and a genuinely different flavor of source-quality issue than in prior topics.** Unlike Topics F, H, M, and L, this topic's external research did not surface a meaningful cluster of unreliable GEO/SEO commercial content — severity-scoring methodology is a mature, well-documented field (cybersecurity vulnerability management) with strong primary and peer-reviewed sources readily available, and the search results reflected that. The one genuine caution this document must apply instead is **domain-transfer risk**: CVSS is a rigorously validated methodology *for its own domain* (software vulnerabilities), and this document found direct, credible, peer-reviewed evidence (arXiv 2412.20087, discussed in §2) that CVSS's own validity has been explicitly and skeptically questioned when applied to a different, newer domain (adversarial attacks against LLMs) — a direct, structural warning against assuming CVSS's specific mechanics transfer cleanly to this hackathon's own novel domain (website-AI-discoverability auditing) without the same kind of critical scrutiny.

## §2 — External precedent: CVSS and confidence-calibration literature

### A. What we need to understand
Does a mature, independently-developed severity-scoring methodology already exist that solves a structurally similar problem (heterogeneous, multi-factor severity assessment, used to prioritize remediation across many findings), and — critically — what does the record of that methodology's own limitations and cross-domain validity teach this document about avoiding the same failure modes, rather than naively adopting its mechanics wholesale?

### B. Why it matters
Building severity-scoring logic entirely from first principles, when a decades-refined, industry-standard precedent already exists for a structurally similar problem, would be a missed opportunity — but adopting that precedent uncritically, without examining its own documented limitations and cross-domain validity questions, would repeat exactly the "borrowed authority without domain fit" mistake this document series has flagged repeatedly elsewhere (Topic T's own rejection of industrial fault-tree/Bayesian-network RCA methods as a poor fit despite being rigorous in their own domain).

### C. Current evidence
- **FACT (industry-standard, extensively documented, directly on point):** the Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS) is a mature, widely-adopted, multi-version-refined (v2 → v3.0 → v3.1 → v4.0) standard for scoring the severity of software vulnerabilities, maintained by FIRST (Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams) and used as the basis for the US National Vulnerability Database's own severity ratings. Its core structural decomposition is directly, non-trivially relevant to this document's own §1 findings: CVSS separates **Base metrics** (a vulnerability's intrinsic, time-invariant characteristics — itself decomposed into **Exploitability** metrics and **Impact** metrics, e.g., the Confidentiality/Integrity/Availability triad), **Temporal metrics** (how the assessment changes over time — e.g., whether exploit code has since become publicly available), and **Environmental metrics** (how the specific deploying organization's own context modifies the score — e.g., asset criticality, existing mitigations). This is a genuinely useful, independently-arrived-at, real-world-validated precedent for exactly this document's own §1 three-axis finding: CVSS's Base/Temporal/Environmental split is structurally analogous to (though not identical to) this document's own evidence-strength / materiality-context / causal-structure three-axis requirement — both are answers to the same underlying design problem (a single severity number cannot represent everything that actually matters for prioritization), arrived at independently, in a completely different domain, decades earlier.
- **FACT, directly and specifically useful, on a mechanism this document's §1 Requirement 4 (Topic T's causal-structure input) can directly borrow from:** CVSS's own patent-literature description (a real vulnerability-risk-analysis system building on CVSS) explicitly distinguishes a **"composite" score** (using "the highest accessibility score and highest impact score across all... vulnerabilities... reflect[ing] that only a single exploited vulnerability is needed... and vulnerabilities... are not independent") from an **"aggregate" score** (a summation across all vulnerabilities, "provid[ing] a measure of the number of options... available to an attacker"). This is a directly transferable, already-solved design pattern for exactly the problem Topic T's jointly-necessary-cluster severity logic needs: the **composite/worst-case logic** (take the maximum, since one sufficient path is enough) is the correct model for Topic T's single-sufficient-cause clusters, while the **aggregate/summation logic** is the correct model for representing overall site-wide risk exposure across many independent clusters — meaning this document doesn't need to invent this distinction from scratch; CVSS's own vulnerability-management literature already names and formally separates it.
- **FACT (peer-reviewed, directly on point, a genuinely important cautionary finding rather than pure endorsement — the domain-transfer warning):** "On the Validity of Traditional Vulnerability Scoring Systems for Adversarial Attacks against LLMs" (arXiv 2412.20087) is a peer-reviewed-adjacent paper that directly investigates whether CVSS (and comparable systems) validly transfers to a newer, structurally different domain (adversarial attacks on LLMs) than the one it was designed for — precisely the kind of critical domain-fit question this document must ask of its own decision to draw on CVSS at all. This paper's existence and explicit skepticism is itself the single most important piece of evidence in this unit: it confirms, from a directly analogous methodological situation (an old, rigorous scoring system being asked to serve a new AI-related domain), that **borrowing a mature scoring framework's structure is reasonable, but borrowing its specific mechanics/weights without validating domain fit is not** — this document accordingly recommends borrowing CVSS's **structural insight** (separate intrinsic/contextual/temporal axes; composite-vs-aggregate logic) while explicitly declining to borrow its **specific formula or numeric weightings**, which were calibrated for a genuinely different kind of harm (confidentiality/integrity/availability of computer systems) than this hackathon's actual concern (AI discoverability/misrepresentation/engagement harm).
- **FACT (peer-reviewed, directly on point, on the confidence/precision side of this unit — a genuinely important, non-obvious finding):** "ZeroFalse: Improving Precision in Static Analysis with LLMs" (arXiv 2510.02534) documents a well-established, structural tension in automated detection tooling directly relevant to this entire marketplace's design: static analysis tools "prioritize soundness over precision by relying on conservative approximations," deliberately over-flagging to avoid missing true issues, which "produces frequent false positives" and, critically, **"severely undermined... practical efficacy... causing 'alert fatigue' and leading to a lack of trust in the tools."** This is a directly relevant, real-world-documented account of exactly the failure mode the brief itself warns against ("a skill that finds 100 mostly meaningless problems is worse than one that finds 12 real problems") — not a hypothetical concern this hackathon invented, but a well-studied, named phenomenon in a mature, adjacent automated-detection field.
- **FACT (peer-reviewed, directly on point, the single most important and genuinely surprising finding in this entire document — a strong, non-obvious warning about calibration measurement itself):** "Confidently Wrong: Severity-Aware Calibration of Prompt-Injection Detectors under Attack Shift" (arXiv 2606.22659) is a rigorous, directly-relevant 2026 paper (studying detector calibration in an LLM-security context, structurally close to this hackathon's own AI-system-adjacent detection problem) that finds detectors can be **confidently, catastrophically wrong on a specific attack subset while appearing well-calibrated overall**: "standard pooled calibration error does not register this; one detector it rates well-calibrated, at 0.06, is miscalibrated at 0.91 on the attacks alone" — meaning an aggregate, whole-system calibration/confidence metric can completely conceal severe, systematic failure on a specific finding *category*, even while the overall number looks reassuring. This is a directly load-bearing, non-obvious methodological warning for Topic S's design: **confidence/calibration must be tracked and reported per finding-category (per skill, or per specific check within a skill), never only as one pooled marketplace-wide statistic** — a pooled "our tool is 85% accurate" claim could be entirely true on average while hiding a specific check (e.g., a specific entity-collision sub-case, or a specific materiality-taxonomy edge case) that is confidently, systematically wrong far more often.
- **OBSERVATION, converging across multiple independent sources in the static-analysis-tooling literature, consistent with and reinforcing this document's own design conclusions:** multiple independent sources (Veracode's documented approach; the ZeroFalse and Buglens papers) confirm that the most effective practical mitigation for the soundness-vs-precision tension is **not a single better threshold, but layered verification** — reachability/context analysis to filter conservative over-approximations, and "cross verification between static, dynamic, and interactive tools" to increase confidence when multiple independent signals agree. This directly reinforces and independently validates (from a completely different research tradition) this document series' own repeatedly-applied hybrid deterministic-first-plus-LLM-escalation design pattern, and specifically validates Topic T's cross-skill co-occurrence flag (Requirement 6/§1) as a genuine confidence-boosting signal, not merely an aggregation convenience: a finding independently corroborated by two different skills' checks (per the CVSS-adjacent multi-signal-agreement literature) should carry demonstrably higher confidence than a finding from a single check alone.

### D. Important mechanisms
Three compounding, evidence-backed design conclusions: **(1) CVSS's structural decomposition (intrinsic/Base, contextual/Environmental, time-varying/Temporal; composite-vs-aggregate logic) is directly, usefully transferable to this document's own §1 three-axis requirement**, giving it independent, real-world-validated precedent rather than treating it as this document's own invention; **(2) CVSS's own specific numeric mechanics should NOT be transferred**, per the directly-analogous domain-validity critique found in the LLM-adversarial-attack literature — borrow structure, not formula; **(3) confidence/calibration must be tracked per-category, never only pooled**, per the "Confidently Wrong" paper's genuinely striking finding that pooled metrics can hide severe, systematic, category-specific miscalibration — a finding this document did not anticipate before researching and treats as a significant, load-bearing addition to the schema's design, not a minor footnote.

### E. Concrete "signals" (schema design inputs)
Not website signals — the "signals" this unit's schema needs to accept as structured input are: (a) an evidence-strength tier per finding (reusing this series' own repeatedly-demonstrated FACT/OBSERVATION/HYPOTHESIS/INFERENCE/SPECULATION legend directly, rather than inventing a new scale — a genuinely elegant, low-cost design choice, since this legend is already used consistently across all eight prior documents and requires no new taxonomy); (b) a materiality-gate result per claim-based finding (Topic P's two-axis taxonomy, directly reused); (c) a causal-structure classification per cluster (Topic T's three-way schema, directly reused); (d) a cross-skill co-occurrence flag (Topic G/J's shared-page findings, directly reused); (e) a per-check-category confidence/calibration tracker, distinct from any pooled marketplace-wide statistic (this document's own new contribution, per the "Confidently Wrong" finding).

### F. How the schema would compute an actual severity/report entry
Deterministic composition function, directly synthesizing every requirement identified: (1) start from Topic T's causal-structure classification for the cluster (single-cause: take the associated finding's own severity; jointly-necessary: take the CVSS-inspired **composite/worst-case** logic — the maximum severity among contributing factors, since per Topic T's own evidence, addressing only a subset may not resolve the symptom, so understating via averaging would be actively misleading; primary-plus-amplifying: weight the primary factor's severity most heavily, with amplifying factors as modifiers, not co-equal contributors); (2) gate the resulting severity through Topic P's materiality taxonomy — a claim failing either axis caps at informational regardless of the causal-structure computation; (3) attach the evidence-strength tier (this series' own standing FACT/OBSERVATION/HYPOTHESIS/INFERENCE/SPECULATION legend) as an independent, always-visible field, never blended into the severity number itself; (4) where a cross-skill co-occurrence flag is present, apply a confidence boost consistent with the multi-signal-agreement literature (§C); (5) track and separately report per-check-category calibration/confidence, never only a pooled marketplace-wide accuracy claim.

### G. What evidence the report should output
For every finding or cluster: severity (via the composition function above), evidence-strength tier (the series' standing legend, always shown, never hidden inside the severity number), materiality-gate result (pass/fail per axis, for claim-based findings), causal-structure classification (for clusters), cross-skill-corroboration flag (boolean plus which skills), and — at the report level, not per-finding — a per-check-category confidence/calibration statement rather than one pooled tool-wide accuracy claim, directly implementing the "Confidently Wrong" paper's core warning.

### H. Possible severity logic
This unit's entire contribution *is* the severity logic (§F) — restated compactly: **severity = materiality-gated, causal-structure-aware composition of contributing findings' impact, reported alongside (never blended with) an independent evidence-strength tier and a per-category confidence statement.**

### I. Correct remediation
N/A directly — this unit governs how remediation *priority* is computed and communicated (via severity) and how remediation *effort* is separately flagged (via Topic T's single-vs-multi-factor modifier), not a remediation of its own.

### J. False-positive cases
The central false-positive risk this entire document exists to prevent, stated at the schema level rather than the individual-check level (where every prior topic has already handled its own version of this): a schema that reports one clean severity number per finding, with no visible evidence-strength or materiality-gate information, risks presenting HYPOTHESIS-tier original-synthesis findings (Topic P's materiality taxonomy itself, Topic T's causal classification, several of Topic M's findings) with the same apparent authority as FACT-tier, peer-reviewed-controlled-experiment findings (Topic F's WhoQA-backed collision detection) — a schema-level false-positive risk (over-trusting a finding) distinct from, and just as important as, any individual check's own website-level false-positive risk.

### K. False-negative risks
Conversely, a schema that hedges every finding's confidence heavily (to avoid the risk above) could suppress genuinely well-evidenced, high-severity findings (like Topic F's WhoQA-backed misattribution risk) down to a level a non-expert user would deprioritize — directly reproducing the brief's own named concern about a report that either overwhelms with noise or, in the opposite failure, buries real signal; the per-category confidence tracking (§C, §F) is specifically designed to prevent this by keeping evidence-strength and severity visibly separate rather than one suppressing the other.

### L. Counterexamples
The "Confidently Wrong" paper's own finding is itself the sharpest counterexample to any assumption that "our tool reports an overall confidence score" is sufficient schema design — it directly demonstrates, in a closely analogous domain, that this assumption can be actively misleading in exactly the cases that matter most (systematic, category-specific failure hiding inside a reassuring aggregate number).

### M. Does this generalize?
The three-axis schema and the CVSS-inspired composite/aggregate distinction are general design principles, independent of website type or specific finding content — this is core reporting infrastructure, not a website-type-dependent check, and is the one topic in this entire series with no direct Topic V (site-type differentiation) hook, since its job is to correctly represent whatever site-type-calibrated findings the other skills produce, not to calibrate anything itself.

### N. Candidate skill(s)
Not a detection skill — this is the **report-schema and severity-composition logic for the marketplace's single entrypoint**, consistent with how every prior topic in this series (most explicitly Topics F, H, P, T, G, and J) has already, independently concluded Topic S's territory should be scoped.
